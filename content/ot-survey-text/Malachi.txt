Absolutely. Continuing the same sequence, the next book is **Malachi** — the final book of the Twelve Minor Prophets and the final book of the Old Testament in the traditional Christian canon.

# MALACHI Survey

# Introduction

The Book of **Malachi** is the final book of the Old Testament in the traditional Christian Bible and the concluding book of the **Twelve Minor Prophets**.

Malachi speaks to the people of Judah after the Babylonian exile. Although the temple had been rebuilt and the people had returned to their land, their spiritual condition had become deeply compromised.

The prophet confronts:

* Spiritual apathy
* Corrupt worship
* Defective sacrifices
* Unfaithful priests
* Marriage covenant violations
* Social injustice
* Doubt concerning God's love
* Neglect of tithes
* Complaints about God's justice
* Lack of reverence for God

The book is structured largely as a series of **questions and answers** between God and His people.

For example:

> “How have you loved us?”
> — **Malachi 1:2**

The people repeatedly question God's statements, and God responds by exposing their spiritual condition.

Malachi therefore functions as a prophetic examination of Israel's covenant faithfulness.

At the same time, the book ends with tremendous hope.

God promises the coming of:

* A messenger who will prepare the way
* The Lord who will come to His temple
* A refiner and purifier
* The “sun of righteousness”
* Elijah before the great and awesome Day of the LORD

These closing promises become especially important when the New Testament begins.

The Gospel writers connect the **messenger who prepares the way** with **John the Baptist**, while Jesus identifies John with the promised Elijah in an important prophetic sense.

Thus Malachi forms a significant bridge between the Old Testament and the New Testament.

---

# Name of the Book

The Hebrew title is:

**מַלְאָכִי — Malʾākhî**

It is commonly understood as:

> **“My messenger”**

or

> **“My angel/messenger.”**

The Hebrew word **מַלְאָךְ (malʾākh)** means:

* Messenger
* Envoy
* Angel

The suffix **-i** means “my.”

Therefore **Malachi** can be understood as:

**“My messenger.”**

## Is Malachi the Prophet's Personal Name?

There is some scholarly discussion concerning this question.

Unlike many prophetic books, Malachi provides very little biographical information.

Malachi 1:1 begins:

> “The oracle of the word of the LORD to Israel by Malachi.”

Some interpreters understand **Malachi** as the prophet's personal name.

Others have suggested that it could function as a designation meaning **“my messenger.”**

The ancient Greek translation treats the word as a proper name, and the traditional Jewish and Christian understanding has generally regarded Malachi as the prophet's name.

For teaching purposes, Malachi can appropriately be treated as the traditional name of the prophet while recognizing the linguistic significance of the name.

---

# Author of the Book

## Traditional Author

The book identifies its messenger as **Malachi**.

However, unlike prophets such as Isaiah, Jeremiah, or Ezekiel, almost nothing is known about his personal life.

There is:

* No genealogy
* No mention of his father
* No hometown
* No extended biography

His authority rests on the phrase:

> “The word of the LORD...”

The focus is therefore not on the prophet's personal story but on **God's message to His covenant people**.

## Internal Evidence

Malachi speaks directly about:

* Priests
* Temple worship
* Sacrifices
* Tithes
* Marriage
* Covenant
* Jerusalem
* Judah
* The coming Day of the LORD

The setting clearly belongs to the **post-exilic community**.

## Scholarly Discussion

Some scholars have proposed that “Malachi” could originally have been a descriptive title rather than a personal name.

Nevertheless, the canonical book presents itself as prophetic revelation delivered through a figure called Malachi, and the traditional attribution remains widely accepted.

---

# Date Written

## Approximate Date

Malachi is generally dated to approximately:

**460–430 BC**

A somewhat narrower date is often proposed around the **mid-fifth century BC**.

The exact year cannot be established with certainty.

## Historical Evidence Within the Book

Several clues point toward the Persian period after the temple had been rebuilt.

The temple was functioning because sacrifices were being offered:

**Malachi 1:7–10**

The priesthood was active:

**Malachi 2:1–9**

The people were neglecting tithes:

**Malachi 3:8–10**

The book also addresses social and marital problems within the community.

## Historical Setting

The Second Temple had already been completed in **516 BC**.

Malachi therefore comes after:

* Haggai
* Zechariah
* The rebuilding of the temple

He probably ministered during the later Persian period.

The broader historical setting overlaps with the era associated with:

* Ezra
* Nehemiah
* Persian rule
* Post-exilic Judah

# Audience / To Whom Was It Written

Malachi was addressed to:

**Israel**, particularly the post-exilic Jewish community in Judah.

His message specifically confronts:

### Priests

**Malachi 1:6–2:9**

### People

**Malachi 2:10–17**

### Those withholding offerings

**Malachi 3:8–12**

### Those questioning God's justice

**Malachi 2:17; 3:13–15**

The prophet addresses the entire covenant community because spiritual decline had affected both religious leaders and ordinary people.

---

# Historical and Geographical Setting

Malachi belongs to the **Persian-period post-exilic community**.

Judah had experienced:

1. Babylonian conquest
2. Destruction of Jerusalem
3. Exile
4. Return under Persian rule
5. Temple rebuilding
6. Restoration of temple worship
7. Gradual spiritual decline

The people were back in the land, but their hearts were not consistently devoted to God.

This distinction is crucial.

**Physical restoration had occurred, but spiritual restoration remained incomplete.**

## Political Setting

Judah was under the authority of the **Persian Empire**.

There is no Jewish king ruling independently in Jerusalem.

Instead, Judah existed as a Persian province.

## Religious Setting

The temple was functioning, but worship had become compromised.

The priests were accepting animals that were:

* Blind
* Lame
* Diseased

See **Malachi 1:8**.

The people were therefore treating worship as a burden rather than a privilege.

## Social Setting

Malachi also addresses:

* Marriage unfaithfulness
* Divorce
* Covenant violations
* Oppression
* Withholding offerings
* Cynicism about God's justice

The problem was not merely ritual.

It involved the whole covenant relationship.

# Purpose of the Book

Malachi has several major purposes.

## 1. To Confront Spiritual Apathy

The people had become religiously active but spiritually careless.

## 2. To Correct Corrupt Worship

The priests were offering unacceptable sacrifices.

## 3. To Call Priests Back to Faithfulness

Religious leadership had failed to honor God's name.

## 4. To Correct Covenant Unfaithfulness

The people were violating their commitments to God and one another.

## 5. To Defend God's Love

The book begins by addressing the people's question:

> “How have you loved us?”

**Malachi 1:2**

## 6. To Correct Wrong Ideas About God's Justice

Some people were asking why evil people seemed to prosper.

See **Malachi 2:17**.

## 7. To Call the People to Faithful Giving

**Malachi 3:8–10** addresses withholding tithes and offerings.

## 8. To Announce God's Coming Judgment

The Day of the LORD is coming.

## 9. To Announce the Coming Messenger

A messenger will prepare the way before the Lord.

**Malachi 3:1**

## 10. To Give Hope to the Faithful

The book ends with the promise of God's ultimate justice and restoration.

# Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

## Chapter 1 – God's Love and Corrupt Worship

Malachi begins with God's declaration:

> “I have loved you.”

Israel questions God's love.

God responds by contrasting Israel with Edom.

### Corrupt Sacrifices

God then confronts the priests.

They are offering animals that are:

* Blind
* Lame
* Sick

The priests would not present such animals to a human governor, yet they present them to God.

### The Central Problem

The people have begun treating worship as:

* Inconvenient
* Boring
* Burdensome

rather than an act of honor toward God.

### Major Lesson

**God deserves reverent and wholehearted worship.**

---

## Chapter 2 – The Priests and Covenant Unfaithfulness

God continues His confrontation with the priests.

The priests were supposed to:

* Teach God's law
* Preserve knowledge
* Lead people toward righteousness

Instead, they had corrupted their ministry.

### Covenant With Levi

God recalls His covenant expectations concerning the priesthood.

### Marriage Covenant

The prophet then confronts the people concerning unfaithfulness in marriage.

Malachi describes marriage as a **covenant**.

The people were acting faithlessly toward their spouses and toward God.

### Major Lesson

Faithfulness to God cannot be separated from faithfulness to people.

---

## Chapter 3 – The Messenger and the Refiner

One of the most important Messianic passages appears:

> “Behold, I send my messenger, and he will prepare the way before me.”

**Malachi 3:1**

The coming Lord will come to His temple.

### The Refiner

The Lord is compared to:

* A refiner's fire
* Fuller's soap

He will purify the sons of Levi.

### Purpose of Purification

God's coming will expose and remove:

* Sin
* Corruption
* Injustice
* False worship

### Social Sins

Malachi identifies several forms of injustice:

* Sorcery
* Adultery
* False testimony
* Oppression of workers
* Oppression of widows
* Oppression of orphans
* Neglect of foreigners

See **Malachi 3:5**.

### Tithes

The people are accused of robbing God by withholding tithes and offerings.

### Faithful Remnant

The chapter ends by describing people who fear the LORD.

God remembers them.

A **book of remembrance** is mentioned.

### Major Lesson

God sees the difference between empty religion and genuine reverence.

---

## Chapter 4 – The Day of the LORD

The final chapter contrasts two destinies.

### The Wicked

The wicked will face judgment.

They are compared to stubble consumed by fire.

### The Righteous

Those who fear God's name receive hope:

> “The sun of righteousness shall rise with healing in its wings.”

### Elijah

God promises to send Elijah before the great and awesome Day of the LORD.

The purpose is relational restoration:

> “And he will turn the hearts of fathers to their children and the hearts of children to their fathers.”

**Malachi 4:6**

The Old Testament ends with this prophetic expectation.

The New Testament then opens with the announcement of the coming Messiah and His forerunner.

# Foreshadows / Types of Jesus Christ

Malachi contains several significant Christological passages.

## 1. The Messenger Who Prepares the Way

**Malachi 3:1**

> “I send my messenger, and he will prepare the way before me.”

The New Testament applies this passage to **John the Baptist**.

### New Testament References

* **Matthew 11:10**
* **Mark 1:2**
* **Luke 7:27**

John functions as the prophetic forerunner who prepares people for Jesus.

---

## 2. The Lord Coming to His Temple

**Malachi 3:1**

The passage speaks of the Lord coming suddenly to His temple.

The New Testament presents Jesus as coming to the temple and exercising authority there.

Examples include:

* **Matthew 21:12–13**
* **Mark 11:15–17**
* **Luke 19:45–46**
* **John 2:13–22**

The precise identification of the “Lord” in Malachi 3:1 should be interpreted within the full biblical context, but Christians have traditionally seen strong Christological significance in the passage.

---

## 3. The Refiner

**Malachi 3:2–3**

The coming Lord purifies His people like a refiner purifies metal.

This connects with the New Testament's teaching concerning Christ's sanctifying and purifying work.

---

## 4. The Sun of Righteousness

**Malachi 4:2**

The “sun of righteousness” is an important image of salvation, healing, and victory.

Christians have historically connected this imagery with Christ, although the text itself does not explicitly identify the figure by the name Jesus.

---

## 5. Elijah and John the Baptist

**Malachi 4:5–6**

Malachi promises Elijah before the Day of the LORD.

The New Testament identifies John the Baptist as coming:

> “in the spirit and power of Elijah.”

**Luke 1:17**

Jesus also identifies John with the promised Elijah in a significant prophetic sense.

See:

* **Matthew 11:14**
* **Matthew 17:10–13**

This does not mean John was literally Elijah reincarnated.

Rather, John fulfilled the **Elijah-like prophetic role** anticipated by Malachi.

# Summary of the Book

Malachi is a prophetic confrontation with a community that had returned from exile physically but had become spiritually complacent.

The people questioned:

* God's love
* God's justice
* The value of serving God

God answered by exposing their:

* Corrupt worship
* Defective sacrifices
* Unfaithful leadership
* Broken relationships
* Social injustice
* Withholding of offerings
* Cynicism

Yet God's message is not merely condemnation.

Malachi points toward hope.

God promises:

**A messenger will come.**

**The Lord will come to His temple.**

**The people will be purified.**

**God will judge wickedness.**

**The faithful will be remembered.**

**The sun of righteousness will rise with healing.**

**Elijah will come before the great Day of the LORD.**

The book therefore ends the Old Testament prophetic story with **expectation**.

Israel is waiting.

The messenger has not yet appeared in Malachi's historical setting.

The promised Lord has not yet brought the final purification.

The great Day of the LORD is still anticipated.

The Old Testament closes with the people looking forward.

# Additional Important Information

## 1. Malachi Uses a Question-and-Answer Structure

One of the most distinctive literary features of Malachi is the repeated dialogue.

The pattern is often:

**God makes a declaration → the people question it → God gives His evidence.**

Examples include:

| God's Statement                        | People's Response                 |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| “I have loved you”                     | “How have you loved us?”          |
| “You despise my name”                  | “How have we despised your name?” |
| “You have wearied the LORD”            | “How have we wearied him?”        |
| “You have robbed me”                   | “How have we robbed you?”         |
| “Your words have been hard against me” | “How have we spoken against you?” |

This structure exposes the gap between **how the people viewed themselves and how God viewed their behavior**.

---

## 2. The Central Problem Was Spiritual Apathy

Malachi's people were not necessarily abandoning religion completely.

Their problem was subtler.

They continued:

* Sacrifices
* Temple worship
* Religious practices

But their hearts had become careless.

This makes Malachi particularly relevant to religious communities.

A person can remain externally religious while becoming internally indifferent.

---

## 3. Malachi and the Priests

The priests had a special responsibility.

They were supposed to:

* Honor God
* Teach truth
* Preserve knowledge
* Lead people in righteousness

Instead, they had become part of the problem.

**Malachi 2:7** says:

> “For the lips of a priest should guard knowledge.”

This principle has continuing significance for Christian teachers and church leaders.

---

## 4. The Famous Tithing Passage

Malachi 3:10 is frequently quoted in Christian discussions about giving.

However, proper interpretation requires recognizing its original context.

Israel was under the Mosaic covenant.

The “storehouse” refers to the temple-centered system through which Israel's tithes supported the Levitical and religious system.

Therefore, the passage should not be transformed into a universal promise that:

> “Give money and God guarantees financial prosperity.”

The broader biblical teaching emphasizes generosity, stewardship, care for others, and cheerful giving.

See:

* **Deuteronomy 14:22–29**
* **2 Corinthians 8–9**
* **2 Corinthians 9:7**

---

## 5. The “Sun of Righteousness”

Malachi 4:2 contains the memorable image:

> “the sun of righteousness shall rise with healing in its wings.”

The image contrasts the darkness of judgment with the joy of salvation.

The Hebrew imagery may be understood in terms of the rising sun and its rays, bringing healing and restoration.

Christian tradition has frequently connected this verse with Christ, but the passage should first be understood within Malachi's contrast between the fate of the wicked and the salvation of those who fear God.

---

## 6. Elijah in Malachi

Malachi's final prophecy concerns Elijah.

This created an important expectation within Judaism.

The New Testament addresses this expectation directly.

John the Baptist is not literally Elijah returned from the dead.

Rather, John comes in:

> “the spirit and power of Elijah.”

**Luke 1:17**

Jesus also speaks of John in relation to Elijah.

**Matthew 11:14**

Thus John fulfills the prophetic role anticipated by Malachi.

---

## 7. Malachi and John the Baptist

This is one of the most important bridges between the Old and New Testaments.

### Malachi

A messenger will prepare the way.

**Malachi 3:1**

### Gospels

John the Baptist prepares the way for Jesus.

* **Matthew 3:1–3**
* **Mark 1:2–4**
* **Luke 3:2–6**
* **John 1:23**

Malachi therefore forms an important prophetic background for the beginning of the Gospel story.

---

## 8. Malachi and the Beginning of the New Testament

There is a remarkable literary-theological movement:

### Malachi ends with:

**Elijah / forerunner → Day of the LORD**

### Luke begins with:

**John the Baptist / forerunner → Jesus**

This creates a strong bridge between the prophetic expectations of the Old Testament and the Gospel proclamation of the New Testament.

---

## 9. The Final Words of the Old Testament

In the traditional Christian arrangement, the Old Testament closes with:

**Malachi 4:6**

The final subject is the coming **Day of the LORD** and the need for covenant restoration.

The Christian Bible then begins the New Testament with the story of Jesus Christ.

This canonical arrangement creates a powerful transition:

**Promise → Waiting → Messiah**

* God's covenant love
* God's holiness
* Worship
* Priesthood

## 11. Malachi's Message for Christian Leaders

Malachi is particularly valuable for pastors, teachers, worship leaders, and church leaders.

Its warning is that religious ministry can become routine.

God does not merely evaluate:

**What are we doing?**

He also evaluates:

**How are we treating His name?**

The priests were performing religious duties while failing to honor God from the heart.

---

## 12. Malachi's Message for the Church

The book challenges believers to examine:

* Why we worship
* How we worship
* How we treat others
* Whether our giving is faithful
* Whether our relationships reflect covenant faithfulness
* Whether we trust God's justice
* Whether we remain faithful when evil seems to prosper

Malachi therefore remains a powerful call to **renewed covenant faithfulness and genuine worship**.

# Conclusion

The Book of Malachi closes the Old Testament with a message that combines **rebuke, repentance, hope, judgment, and expectation**.

God's people had returned from exile, but their spiritual condition remained troubled.

The priests had corrupted worship.

The people had become spiritually indifferent.

Marriage covenants were being violated.

The vulnerable were being oppressed.

Tithes and offerings were being withheld.

Some questioned whether serving God was worthwhile.

Yet God had not forgotten His people.

He declared:

> “I have loved you.”

And He promised that the day would come when He would intervene decisively.

A messenger would prepare the way.

The Lord would come to His temple.

He would purify His people.

He would judge evil.

He would remember those who feared Him.

The sun of righteousness would rise with healing.

And Elijah would come before the great and awesome Day of the LORD.

For Christians, these promises become especially significant when read alongside the New Testament.

**John the Baptist** appears as the prophetic forerunner.

**Jesus Christ** appears as the promised Messiah and Lord.

The New Testament therefore begins to answer the expectation with which Malachi ends.

### Central Message of Malachi

> **God calls His people to wholehearted covenant faithfulness, promises to purify and judge, remembers those who fear Him, and announces the coming of His messenger and the Lord who brings salvation and final justice.**

### One-Sentence Survey

**Malachi is God's final Old Testament call to covenant faithfulness, ending with the promise of a coming messenger, the Lord's arrival, the purification of His people, and the great Day of the LORD.**
