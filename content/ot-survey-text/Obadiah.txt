# Introduction

## Historical Connection to Amos

As we continue through the Minor Prophets, we move from **Amos** to **Obadiah**.

While Amos warned **Israel (the Northern Kingdom)** about injustice and coming judgment, **Obadiah** focuses on **Edom**, Israel's neighboring nation and long-standing enemy.

The sequence of these books reveals an important truth:

* **Amos** teaches that God's own people are accountable to Him.
* **Obadiah** teaches that the surrounding nations are also accountable.
* God judges impartially, whether the people are Israelites or Gentiles.

---

# Historical Background

To understand Obadiah, we must go back to the family of **Isaac**.

Isaac had twin sons:

* **Jacob** (later called Israel)
* **Esau**

Although they were brothers, conflict began before their birth (Genesis 25).

Esau eventually became the father of the nation of **Edom**.

Jacob became the father of **Israel**.

The hostility between the brothers continued through their descendants for centuries.

---

## Israel and Edom

Throughout the Old Testament, the relationship between Israel and Edom was often marked by conflict.

Some important events include:

* Edom refused Israel permission to pass through its land during the Exodus (Numbers 20:14–21).
* Saul fought against Edom.
* David conquered Edom (2 Samuel 8:13–14).
* Edom later rebelled against Judah.
* During the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem, the Edomites rejoiced over Judah's downfall and participated in violence against fleeing survivors (Psalm 137:7).

This betrayal by a "brother nation" forms the background of Obadiah.

---

# When Did Obadiah Live?

Unlike many prophetic books, Obadiah gives almost no information about the prophet himself.

Two major views exist regarding the date:

| View                                 | Approximate Date | Historical Setting                                               |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Early Date**                       | c. 845 BC        | During conflicts between Judah and Edom in the reign of Jehoram. |
| **Late Date (Most Widely Accepted)** | **586–550 BC**   | Shortly after the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem.           |

### Preferred View

Most evangelical scholars favor the **late-date view** because Obadiah's description closely matches the events surrounding **Jerusalem's fall in 586 BC**.

Edom not only celebrated Judah's defeat but also:

* Entered Jerusalem.
* Plundered the city.
* Captured fleeing refugees.
* Handed survivors over to Babylon.

These actions explain the severity of God's judgment announced in Obadiah.

## What Is the Book About?

Obadiah is God's declaration of judgment against **Edom** because of its pride and violence.

The Edomites believed their mountain fortresses made them invincible.

Their capital, built among steep cliffs, seemed impossible to conquer.

God declares:

> Even if you make your home among the stars, I will bring you down.

The message is clear:

Human pride can never stand against God's sovereignty.

Yet Obadiah is not only about judgment.

The book ends with hope.

God promises that:

* His kingdom will triumph.
* His people will be restored.
* Justice will prevail.

---

## Placement in the Bible

* Old Testament.
* Fourth book of the Twelve Minor Prophets.
* 31st book of the Bible.

---

## Historical Importance

Obadiah is significant because it:

* Explains God's judgment upon Edom.
* Demonstrates that nations are accountable for how they treat God's people.
* Introduces the theme of the **Day of the LORD** as a day of justice for all nations.
* Assures believers that pride and oppression will not ultimately prevail.
* Affirms God's sovereign rule over history.

---

## Main Focus of the Book

The central message of Obadiah is:

> **God opposes pride, judges those who persecute His people, and promises the ultimate triumph of His kingdom.**

---

## Why This Book Matters to Christians Today

Obadiah remains relevant because it teaches:

* Pride leads to destruction.
* God sees every act of injustice.
* No nation or individual is beyond God's authority.
* Believers can trust God to bring perfect justice.
* God's kingdom will ultimately prevail over every earthly power.

# Name of the Book

## Original Hebrew Name

**עֹבַדְיָה (ʿŌvaḏyāh)**

---

## Root Word

The name comes from the Hebrew root **עבד (ʿavad)**, meaning:

* To serve.
* To work.
* To worship.

Combined with **Yah (Yahweh)**, the name means:

**"Servant of Yahweh"** or **"Worshiper of the LORD."**

---

## Literal Meaning

**Obadiah** means:

> **"Servant of the LORD."**

This is one of the most common names in the Old Testament. More than a dozen individuals bear this name, but the prophet Obadiah is distinguished by the book that bears his name.

---

## Why the Book Is Called by This Name

The book is named after **Obadiah**, the prophet through whom God delivered this message concerning Edom.

Although little is known about his personal life, his name reflects his role as a faithful servant entrusted with proclaiming God's justice.

---

# Author of the Book

## Traditional Author

The traditional author is **Obadiah** (Obadiah 1:1).

Beyond his name, Scripture provides no personal details about him.

Unlike prophets such as Isaiah or Jeremiah, Obadiah gives no genealogy, hometown, or record of his occupation.

His message—not his biography—is the focus of the book.

---

## Internal Evidence

The book opens with:

> **"The vision of Obadiah."**

The prophecy is presented as a divine revelation received from the Lord concerning Edom.

The consistent style and unified message support single authorship.

---

## External Historical Evidence

Jewish and Christian tradition have consistently accepted Obadiah as the author.

Although several other men named Obadiah appear elsewhere in the Old Testament, there is no convincing evidence that any of them wrote this prophetic book.

---

## Scholarly Opinions

Scholars generally agree that:

* Obadiah wrote the book.
* The principal debate concerns **when** he lived rather than **who** he was.

Most modern evangelical scholars place the book shortly after the destruction of Jerusalem in **586 BC**, while acknowledging that an earlier date has also been proposed.

## Important Historical Background During the Writing Period

### The Fall of Jerusalem

In **586 BC**, the Babylonians under King **Nebuchadnezzar II** destroyed Jerusalem.

The city was:

* Captured.
* Plundered.
* Burned.
* Its temple destroyed.
* Many inhabitants taken into exile.

Rather than helping their relatives in Judah, the Edomites took advantage of the tragedy.

According to Obadiah, Edom:

* Rejoiced over Judah's disaster.
* Entered the ruined city.
* Shared in its plunder.
* Captured fleeing survivors.
* Delivered refugees to the enemy.

Because Edom acted with pride and cruelty against a "brother nation," God announced severe judgment upon them.

# Historical and Geographical Setting

## Historical Context

The long-standing conflict between **Jacob** and **Esau** forms the backdrop of Obadiah.

Although the brothers were reconciled personally (Genesis 33), conflict continued between their descendants.

For centuries, Edom repeatedly opposed Israel.

The climax came when Jerusalem fell to Babylon and Edom celebrated Judah's destruction instead of helping.

---

## Political Background

At the time of Obadiah:

### Babylon

* The dominant world empire.
* Destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BC.
* Took many Judeans into exile.

### Judah

* Defeated and devastated.
* Temple destroyed.
* Monarchy ended.
* Many citizens deported.

### Edom

* Independent.
* Allied itself with Babylon's interests.
* Expected to profit from Judah's collapse.

God declared that Edom's apparent security would not last.

---

## Cultural Setting

### Edom's Pride

Edom lived in mountainous territory with naturally fortified cities.

Their rock strongholds gave them great confidence.

They believed:

* No army could conquer them.
* Their location guaranteed safety.
* Their wealth would endure.

God challenged this pride by declaring that He Himself would bring them down.

---

## Religious Condition

Like many neighboring nations, Edom worshiped various pagan deities.

The nation rejected the God of Israel and trusted instead in:

* Military strength.
* Political alliances.
* Wealth.
* Geographic defenses.

Obadiah demonstrates that no false security can stand against the Lord.

# Key Verses

Although Obadiah contains only **21 verses**, it is rich with theological truth. The following verses summarize the book's major themes of pride, justice, the Day of the LORD, and God's kingdom.

---

## Obadiah 1:3–4

> **"The pride of your heart has deceived you, you who dwell in the clefts of the rock... Though you soar aloft like the eagle... from there I will bring you down, declares the LORD."**

### Explanation

Edom trusted in its mountain fortresses and believed no enemy could conquer it. God declares that no earthly security can protect those who exalt themselves against Him.

### Why It Is Significant

* Warns against pride and self-reliance.
* Demonstrates God's sovereignty over nations.
* Reminds believers that true security is found only in God.

---

## Obadiah 1:10

> **"Because of the violence done to your brother Jacob, shame shall cover you, and you shall be cut off forever."**

### Explanation

God identifies Edom's greatest sin as violence against its "brother," Judah. Their actions violated both family ties and basic principles of justice.

### Why It Is Significant

* Shows God's concern for justice.
* Teaches accountability for mistreating others.
* Highlights the seriousness of betraying those in need.

---

## Obadiah 1:15

> **"For the day of the LORD is near upon all the nations. As you have done, it shall be done to you; your deeds shall return on your own head."**

### Explanation

This verse introduces the universal scope of the **Day of the LORD**. God's judgment extends beyond Edom to every nation that opposes His righteousness.

### Why It Is Significant

* Declares God's impartial justice.
* Introduces the biblical principle of divine retribution.
* Points forward to God's final judgment.

---

## Obadiah 1:17

> **"But on Mount Zion there shall be those who escape, and it shall be holy, and the house of Jacob shall possess their own possessions."**

### Explanation

After announcing judgment, God promises deliverance and restoration for His covenant people.

### Why It Is Significant

* Reveals God's covenant faithfulness.
* Balances judgment with hope.
* Anticipates God's future kingdom.

---

## Obadiah 1:21

> **"Saviors shall go up to Mount Zion to rule Mount Esau, and the kingdom shall be the LORD's."**

### Explanation

The book concludes by declaring God's ultimate reign over all creation.

### Why It Is Significant

* Affirms God's absolute sovereignty.
* Looks forward to the Messiah's universal kingdom.
* Provides hope beyond present circumstances.

# Verse-by-Verse / Section-by-Section Summary

---

## Verses 1–4 – Pride Brings Down Edom

### Main Events

Obadiah begins with a vision announcing that God has summoned the nations against Edom.

Although Edom considers itself secure because of its mountain strongholds, God declares that pride has deceived the nation.

Even if Edom could rise as high as an eagle or build among the stars, God Himself would bring it down.

---

### Important Teachings

* Pride blinds people to reality.
* Earthly security cannot resist God's judgment.
* God humbles those who exalt themselves.

---

### Spiritual Lessons

* Trust God rather than personal strength.
* Pride always leads to downfall.
* Humility is essential for those who seek the Lord.

---

## Verses 5–9 – Edom's False Confidence Exposed

### Main Events

God compares Edom's coming destruction with thieves and grape gatherers.

Ordinary thieves leave something behind, but Edom's judgment will be complete.

Their allies will betray them.

Their famous wise men will lose understanding.

Their mighty warriors will be powerless.

Everything in which Edom trusted will fail.

---

### Important Teachings

* Human wisdom cannot replace God's wisdom.
* Political alliances are unreliable.
* Military strength cannot save a nation under God's judgment.

---

### Spiritual Lessons

* Do not place ultimate confidence in wealth, intelligence, or power.
* God alone is our refuge.
* Earthly securities are temporary.

---

## Verses 10–14 – Edom's Sins Against Judah

### Main Events

This section explains why God judges Edom.

Their sins include:

* Violence against Judah.
* Standing by while Jerusalem was attacked.
* Rejoicing over Judah's suffering.
* Boasting during Judah's distress.
* Entering Jerusalem to plunder.
* Capturing fleeing refugees.
* Delivering survivors to the enemy.

Rather than helping their relatives, the Edomites took advantage of their weakness.

---

### Important Teachings

* God condemns indifference to suffering.
* Rejoicing over another person's downfall is sinful.
* Betrayal carries serious consequences.

---

### Spiritual Lessons

* Show compassion to those in distress.
* Never profit from another person's suffering.
* Love your neighbor, even in difficult circumstances.

---

## Verses 15–16 – The Day of the LORD

### Main Events

The prophecy broadens from Edom to **all nations**.

God declares that the Day of the LORD is near.

The principle of divine justice is clear:

"As you have done, it shall be done to you."

The nations that opposed God and His people will experience His righteous judgment.

---

### Important Teachings

* God judges every nation impartially.
* Sin ultimately brings consequences.
* Divine justice is certain.

---

### Spiritual Lessons

* Live with the awareness that God sees every action.
* Extend mercy because God values justice.
* Trust God rather than seeking personal revenge.

---

The book concludes with hope.

* Deliverance on Mount Zion.

---

### Important Teachings

* God's purposes end in restoration.
* His covenant promises never fail.
* God's kingdom will triumph over every earthly kingdom.

---

### Spiritual Lessons

* God's judgment is never His final word for His people.
* Believers can confidently await God's eternal kingdom.
* Hope rests in God's faithfulness, not in present circumstances.

| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------- | --------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Edom will be brought low because of pride | Obadiah           | Obadiah 1:1–9   | Fulfilled as Edom declined and eventually disappeared from history.                           |
| Edom judged for violence against Judah    | Obadiah           | Obadiah 1:10–14 | Fulfilled through successive historical judgments and the nation's downfall.                  |
| The Day of the LORD upon all nations      | Obadiah           | Obadiah 1:15–16 | Partially reflected in historical judgments and ultimately fulfilled in God's final judgment. |

---

# Summary of the Book

The Book of Obadiah is a concise but powerful proclamation of God's justice. Through the prophet Obadiah, the Lord announces judgment against Edom for its pride, violence, and betrayal of Judah during Jerusalem's darkest hour.

The book reminds readers that no nation is beyond God's authority and that pride inevitably leads to downfall. At the same time, it offers hope by assuring God's people that He remains faithful to His covenant and will restore His kingdom.

Though brief, Obadiah powerfully declares that history is moving toward the day when **"the kingdom shall be the LORD's."**

---

## Main Theological Message

**God opposes pride, defends His covenant people, judges evil with perfect justice, and will establish His everlasting kingdom.**

---

## Spiritual Takeaway

* Walk humbly before God.
* Trust His justice rather than seeking revenge.
* Show compassion instead of taking advantage of others.
* Place your confidence in God's kingdom rather than earthly security.
* Live with hope, knowing that God's purposes will prevail.

---

## Importance in the Bible

Obadiah is significant because it:

* Demonstrates God's impartial justice among all nations.
* Warns against pride and betrayal.
* Introduces key themes of the Day of the LORD.
* Reinforces God's faithfulness to His covenant.
* Points forward to the universal reign of the Messiah.

---

# Additional Important Information

## Literary Structure

Obadiah follows a simple yet powerful progression:

1. **Judgment Announced** (vv. 1–9)
2. **Reason for Judgment** (vv. 10–14)
3. **The Day of the LORD** (vv. 15–16)
4. **Hope and Restoration** (vv. 17–21)

This movement from **judgment** to **hope** mirrors the pattern found in many prophetic books.

---

## Covenants

Obadiah reflects God's ongoing faithfulness to:

* The **Abrahamic Covenant**, through His commitment to Abraham's descendants.
* The **Davidic Covenant**, by anticipating the future reign of God's kingdom centered on Zion.

---

## Historical Significance

The downfall of Edom fulfilled Obadiah's prophecy and illustrates that even seemingly secure nations are accountable to God. Over time, Edom was absorbed by surrounding peoples and disappeared as an independent nation.

---

## Hebrew Insights

* **Obadiah (עֹבַדְיָה)** means **"Servant of Yahweh."**
* **Edom (אֱדוֹם)** means **"Red,"** recalling Esau's connection with the red stew in Genesis 25.
* **Zion (צִיּוֹן)** represents God's chosen dwelling place and the hope of His redeemed people.

---

## Connections to the Gospel

Obadiah prepares readers for the Gospel by emphasizing:

* God's righteous judgment against sin.
* Humanity's need for humility and repentance.
* The certainty of God's coming kingdom.
* The ultimate reign of Jesus Christ over all nations.

---

## Archaeological Insights

The ancient Edomite capital of **Sela** (later associated with **Petra**) and other mountain fortresses demonstrate why Edom trusted in its natural defenses. These rugged strongholds illustrate the background of Obadiah's warning that no earthly refuge can withstand God's judgment.

---

## Important Study Notes for Bible Teachers

* Explain the family relationship between **Jacob** and **Esau** to understand the depth of Edom's betrayal.
* Compare Obadiah with **Jeremiah 49:7–22** and **Ezekiel 35**, which also contain prophecies against Edom.
* Highlight how the **Day of the LORD** includes both judgment and salvation.
* Emphasize that God's justice is impartial—He judges both His covenant people and the surrounding nations.
* Show how the book concludes with hope, directing attention to God's sovereign kingdom.

---

# Conclusion

Though only **21 verses** long, Obadiah delivers a timeless message. It warns that pride, violence, and indifference to the suffering of others invite God's judgment, while humility and trust in the Lord lead to lasting hope.

The book assures believers that God sees every injustice and remains faithful to His promises. Its final declaration—**"the kingdom shall be the LORD's"**—looks beyond Edom and Judah to the ultimate reign of God through the Messiah.

For pastors, teachers, students, and all believers, Obadiah reminds us that **God's justice is certain, His covenant is secure, and His kingdom will stand forever.**

---

## Obadiah Survey Complete

**Next Book:** Jonah

Jonah shifts the focus from judgment on Israel's neighbors to **God's compassion for the nations**. Unlike Obadiah, where a foreign nation is condemned, Jonah reveals God's desire to extend mercy even to Israel's enemies when they genuinely repent. It is one of the Old Testament's clearest demonstrations of God's missionary heart and foreshadows the universal scope of the Gospel.
