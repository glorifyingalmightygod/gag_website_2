# HABAKKUK Survey

# Introduction

The **Book of Habakkuk** is the eighth book among the Twelve Minor Prophets in the traditional Christian ordering of the Old Testament. It contains **three chapters** and is distinctive because, rather than primarily delivering God's message to the people, Habakkuk records a profound **conversation between the prophet and God**.

Habakkuk begins by asking difficult questions:

* Why does God allow violence and injustice?
* Why does God appear silent when His people suffer?
* Why would God use a nation even more wicked than Judah to accomplish judgment?
* How should God's people respond when they cannot understand His ways?

The book moves from **confusion to faith, from complaint to confidence, and from fear to worship**.

A central statement of the book is:

> **"The righteous shall live by his faith." — Habakkuk 2:4**

This verse became enormously important in the New Testament and is quoted in **Romans 1:17, Galatians 3:11, and Hebrews 10:38**.

Habakkuk is especially valuable because it does not pretend that difficult questions about suffering and God's sovereignty are easy. Instead, it teaches believers how to bring those questions honestly before God while continuing to trust Him.

## Main Focus of Habakkuk

The major movement of the book can be summarized as:

**Question → God's Answer → Deeper Question → God's Revelation → Faith → Worship**

Habakkuk does not receive every explanation he wants. Instead, he receives a greater revelation of **who God is**.

## Relevance for Christians Today

Habakkuk speaks powerfully to believers who experience:

* injustice
* unanswered prayer
* suffering
* social corruption
* uncertainty about the future
* apparent silence from God
* difficult circumstances
* confusion about God's methods
* fear about what is coming

The final chapter gives one of Scripture's strongest pictures of **faith that worships God even when circumstances do not improve**.

# Name of the Book

## Hebrew Name

The Hebrew name is:

**חֲבַקּוּק — Ḥăvaqqûq / Habakkuk**

The precise etymology of the name is debated.

It is commonly connected with the Hebrew root **חבק (ḥ-b-q)**, associated with the idea of **embracing**.

Because of this possible connection, the name has sometimes been understood in the sense of:

* "embrace"
* "one who embraces"
* possibly "the embraced one"

However, the exact origin of the name remains uncertain, and some scholars have proposed connections with an Akkadian term.

## Why Is the Book Called Habakkuk?

The book is named after its prophet:

> **"The oracle that Habakkuk the prophet saw." — Habakkuk 1:1**

Unlike some prophetic books that contain extensive biographical information, Habakkuk tells us very little about the prophet's personal background.

His name is preserved because his spiritual struggle and prophetic message became part of Israel's Scriptures.

---

# Author of the Book

## Traditional Author

The traditional author is **Habakkuk the prophet**.

Habakkuk 1:1 explicitly identifies him:

> "The oracle that Habakkuk the prophet saw."

Habakkuk 3:1 also identifies the final section as:

> "A prayer of Habakkuk the prophet, according to Shigionoth."

Therefore, the biblical text itself associates the book with Habakkuk.

## Internal Evidence

The book presents Habakkuk as someone who:

* receives a divine revelation
* questions God concerning Judah's violence
* listens for God's response
* records God's answer
* receives a revelation concerning the future
* composes a prayer/song of worship

Habakkuk's role is unusual because much of the book consists of **dialogue between the prophet and God**.

## External Historical Evidence

Habakkuk became part of the collection known as the **Twelve Minor Prophets**.

An important ancient witness is the **Habakkuk Commentary from Qumran**, commonly called the **Pesher Habakkuk**, which demonstrates that Habakkuk was regarded as an authoritative prophetic text in the Second Temple period.

## Scholarly Considerations

There is broad agreement that the book centers on the prophetic figure Habakkuk.

Some scholarly discussion concerns:

* the precise date of composition
* the historical setting of the Chaldean/Babylonian threat
* the relationship between chapters 1–2 and the liturgical prayer of chapter 3
* the meaning of certain Hebrew terms
* whether portions of chapter 3 were adapted for later worship

Nevertheless, the canonical book presents all three chapters as the prophetic witness of Habakkuk.

# Date Written

The exact date is debated, but Habakkuk is generally placed in the **late seventh century BC**, during the rise of Babylon.

A reasonable approximate range is:

**c. 610–600 BC**

Some scholars narrow the setting further, commonly placing it during the reign of **Jehoiakim (609–598 BC)**.

# Audience / To Whom Was It Written

Habakkuk's immediate historical setting is **Judah**.

The prophet is deeply concerned about the condition of his own nation:

> "Why do you make me see iniquity, and why do you idly look at wrong?" — Habakkuk 1:3

The book therefore speaks to:

* the people of Judah
* God's covenant community
* those struggling with injustice
* those questioning God's apparent silence
* believers facing uncertain circumstances

The message ultimately extends beyond Judah because the theological questions are universal:

**How should God's people trust Him when His ways are difficult to understand?**

---

# Historical and Geographical Setting

Habakkuk ministered during a period of enormous political and spiritual upheaval.

## 1. Judah's Internal Problems

Habakkuk describes:

* violence
* injustice
* wrongdoing
* oppression
* disputes
* corrupted justice

Habakkuk 1:2–4 describes a society where:

> "the law is paralyzed, and justice never goes forth."

The prophet therefore begins by asking why God has not intervened.

## 2. The Rise of Babylon

God's answer is startling.

God says:

> "For behold, I am raising up the Chaldeans." — Habakkuk 1:6

The **Chaldeans** are generally understood here as the Babylonian imperial power.

Babylon was becoming one of the dominant powers of the ancient Near East.

## 3. The Fall of Assyria

The Assyrian Empire had previously dominated the region.

Nineveh, the Assyrian capital, fell in **612 BC**.

This dramatically changed the political landscape.

Habakkuk therefore belongs to a period of transition:

**Assyria declines → Babylon rises → Judah faces a new threat.**

## 4. The Spiritual Problem

Habakkuk's greatest struggle is not simply political.

It is theological:

**How can a holy God use a nation more wicked than Judah as an instrument of judgment?**

Habakkuk 1:13 asks:

> "You who are of purer eyes than to see evil and cannot look at wrong, why do you idly look at those who deal treacherously?"

This becomes the central theological tension of the book.

The geographical references in chapter 3 function largely within **poetic and theological imagery**, recalling God's mighty acts in Israel's history.

# Purpose of the Book

Habakkuk serves several important purposes.

## 1. To Address the Problem of Evil

The book openly asks why God permits injustice.

## 2. To Teach Faith in God's Sovereignty

Habakkuk learns that God is working even when His work is difficult to understand.

## 3. To Reveal God's Justice

God will judge both Judah and Babylon.

Being used by God as an instrument of judgment does not make Babylon morally innocent.

## 4. To Teach Righteous Living by Faith

Habakkuk 2:4 becomes the theological center of the book:

> "The righteous shall live by his faith."

## 5. To Move God's People from Fear to Worship

The prophet begins with questions and ends with worship.

## 6. To Demonstrate Biblical Lament

Habakkuk teaches that believers can honestly bring difficult questions before God without abandoning faith.

# Key Verses

## Habakkuk 1:5

> **"Look among the nations, and see; wonder and be astounded. For I am doing a work in your days that you would not believe if told."**

### Explanation

God tells Habakkuk that He is working, even though the prophet does not understand what He is doing.

### Significance

God's activity may exceed human expectations.

---

## Habakkuk 1:13

> **"You who are of purer eyes than to see evil and cannot look at wrong..."**

### Explanation

Habakkuk recognizes God's holiness but struggles to understand how God can use Babylon.

### Significance

This verse captures the theological tension at the center of the book.

---

## Habakkuk 2:4

> **"But the righteous shall live by his faith."**

### Explanation

The Hebrew word commonly translated "faith" can also carry the sense of **faithfulness, steadfastness, or firmness**.

The verse contrasts the proud person with the person who trusts God.

### New Testament Significance

This verse is quoted in:

* Romans 1:17
* Galatians 3:11
* Hebrews 10:38

It became foundational to the biblical theology of faith and righteousness.

---

## Habakkuk 2:14

> **"For the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD as the waters cover the sea."**

### Explanation

Human empires may rise and fall, but God's glory is the ultimate reality.

### Significance

God's purposes extend far beyond the immediate historical crisis.

---

## Habakkuk 2:20

> **"But the LORD is in his holy temple; let all the earth keep silence before him."**

### Explanation

The nations may boast, conquer, and build empires, but God remains sovereign.

### Significance

The verse calls humanity to humility before God.

---

## Habakkuk 3:17–18

> **"Though the fig tree should not blossom, nor fruit be on the vines... yet I will rejoice in the LORD; I will take joy in the God of my salvation."**

### Explanation

Habakkuk describes circumstances in which agricultural prosperity disappears.

Yet his joy is no longer dependent upon circumstances.

### Significance

This is one of Scripture's clearest expressions of **faith that rejoices in God despite suffering and uncertainty**.

# Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

## Chapter 1 – Habakkuk's Questions and God's Unexpected Answer

### Main Events

Habakkuk begins with a complaint.

He sees:

* violence
* injustice
* destruction
* conflict
* corrupted justice

He asks:

> "O LORD, how long shall I cry for help, and you will not hear?" — Habakkuk 1:2

Habakkuk expects God to intervene.

Instead, God announces something unexpected:

> "I am raising up the Chaldeans." — Habakkuk 1:6

The Babylonians will be used as an instrument of judgment.

But this creates an even greater problem for Habakkuk.

### Habakkuk's Second Question

Babylon appears even more wicked than Judah.

Habakkuk therefore asks how a holy God can tolerate such a nation.

### Spiritual Lessons

* Believers can honestly bring difficult questions to God.
* God's silence does not necessarily mean God's inactivity.
* God's answers may be unexpected.
* Human beings do not always understand God's methods.
* Faith does not require pretending that difficult questions do not exist.

---

## Chapter 2 – The Righteous Shall Live by Faith

Habakkuk positions himself to wait for God's answer.

> "I will take my stand at my watchpost." — Habakkuk 2:1

God tells him to write the vision clearly.

### The Vision

The message concerns God's appointed time.

Habakkuk 2:3 emphasizes that the vision awaits its appointed time and calls God's people to wait.

Then comes the central statement:

> **"The righteous shall live by his faith." — Habakkuk 2:4**

The proud person trusts himself.

The righteous person trusts God.

### Five Woes Against Babylon

Habakkuk 2 contains a series of judgments against the oppressive empire.

#### 1. Woe Against Exploitation

Habakkuk 2:6–8

Babylon accumulated wealth through conquest and exploitation.

#### 2. Woe Against Greedy Ambition

Habakkuk 2:9–11

Babylon attempted to establish security through unjust gain.

#### 3. Woe Against Violent Empire-Building

Habakkuk 2:12–14

The empire was built through bloodshed.

Yet God's glory will ultimately fill the earth.

#### 4. Woe Against Shameful Oppression

Habakkuk 2:15–17

The imagery describes exploitation and humiliation.

God promises that violence will return upon the violent.

#### 5. Woe Against Idolatry

Habakkuk 2:18–20

Idols cannot speak, teach, or save.

In contrast:

> "The LORD is in his holy temple."

### Spiritual Lessons

* God's timing is trustworthy even when it seems slow.
* Pride eventually comes under judgment.
* Oppressive power is temporary.
* Faith means trusting God while waiting.
* Human idols cannot replace the living God.
* God's glory is the ultimate goal of history.

---

## Chapter 3 – From Fear to Faithful Worship

Chapter 3 is a prayer of Habakkuk.

The prophet remembers God's mighty acts.

He describes God appearing in majestic imagery involving:

* mountains
* storms
* light
* trembling
* God's mighty power
* God's judgment over enemies

The language recalls God's great acts in Israel's history.

### Habakkuk's Fear

Habakkuk says:

> "I hear, and my body trembles." — Habakkuk 3:16

He understands that difficult days are coming.

### Habakkuk's Declaration of Faith

Then comes the climax:

> "Though the fig tree should not blossom..."

Even if:

* crops fail
* vines produce nothing
* olive harvests disappear
* fields yield no food
* sheep disappear
* cattle are absent

Habakkuk says:

> **"Yet I will rejoice in the LORD." — Habakkuk 3:18**

His circumstances have not changed.

**His understanding of God has changed.**

### Final Statement

> "GOD, the Lord, is my strength." — Habakkuk 3:19

### Spiritual Lessons

* Remembering God's past faithfulness strengthens present faith.
* Worship is possible even during suffering.
* Joy in God is deeper than joy in circumstances.
* Faith does not require certainty about tomorrow.
* God Himself is the believer's strength.

# Theological Themes

## 1. The Sovereignty of God

God is sovereign over:

* Judah
* Babylon
* nations
* history
* judgment
* salvation
* the future

## 2. The Problem of Evil

Habakkuk directly addresses the apparent tension between:

* God's holiness
* human wickedness
* God's sovereignty
* suffering

## 3. Divine Justice

God judges Judah's injustice.

God also judges Babylon.

Therefore, divine sovereignty never means that evil is morally approved.

## 4. Faith

Habakkuk 2:4 is the central theological statement.

Faith involves trusting God and remaining steadfast in Him.

## 5. God's Timing

Habakkuk learns that God's purposes operate according to His appointed time.

## 6. Human Pride

Babylon represents arrogant human power.

Habakkuk 2 repeatedly exposes the danger of pride.

## 7. Judgment

God's judgment is not limited to one nation.

Both Judah and Babylon are accountable to Him.

## 8. God's Glory

Habakkuk 2:14 presents a magnificent vision:

> "The earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD."

## 9. Worship

The book ends not with an explanation of every problem but with worship.

## 10. Hope

Hope is rooted ultimately in God rather than in circumstances.

# Foreshadows / Types of Jesus Christ

Habakkuk does not contain a direct messianic prophecy comparable to passages such as Micah 5:2 or Isaiah 53.

However, the book has significant **Christological connections**, particularly through its teaching about faith and righteousness.

## 1. Habakkuk 2:4 and the Gospel

> "The righteous shall live by his faith."

This verse is quoted three times in the New Testament:

* **Romans 1:17**
* **Galatians 3:11**
* **Hebrews 10:38**

Paul uses Habakkuk 2:4 in explaining the gospel and righteousness by faith.

### Romans 1:17

Paul declares that:

> "the righteous shall live by faith."

The verse therefore becomes an important foundation for understanding the New Testament message concerning faith and righteousness.

## 2. Faith During the Waiting Period

Habakkuk teaches God's people to live faithfully while waiting for God's appointed purposes.

This resonates with the New Testament call for believers to:

* persevere
* remain faithful
* wait for Christ
* trust God's promises

See:

* Hebrews 10:35–39
* James 5:7–8
* 2 Peter 3:8–14

## 3. God's Glory Filling the Earth

Habakkuk 2:14 points toward the universal scope of God's glory.

The New Testament presents Christ's kingdom and gospel as extending among **all nations**.

See:

* Matthew 28:18–20
* Philippians 2:9–11
* Revelation 7:9–10

## 4. God as Strength and Salvation

Habakkuk concludes with joy in:

> "the God of my salvation." — Habakkuk 3:18

The New Testament reveals salvation ultimately and decisively through Jesus Christ.

See:

* Luke 2:30–32
* Acts 4:12
* Titus 2:11–14

# Summary of the Book

Habakkuk begins with a troubled prophet looking at a troubled society.

He asks:

**"How long?"**

God answers that He is already working.

But God's answer creates another question:

**How can a holy God use Babylon to judge Judah?**

God responds by revealing that Babylon itself will also face judgment.

Then comes the central principle:

> **"The righteous shall live by his faith." — Habakkuk 2:4**

The prophet eventually moves from questioning God's actions to trusting God's character.

By chapter 3, Habakkuk knows that severe circumstances are coming.

Yet he declares:

> **"Yet I will rejoice in the LORD."**

The book therefore traces a remarkable spiritual transformation:

**Complaint → Conversation → Revelation → Faith → Worship**

# Additional Important Information

## 1. Habakkuk Is Different from Many Prophetic Books

Many prophetic books primarily contain messages delivered **from God to the people**.

Habakkuk contains significant dialogue **between the prophet and God**.

This makes the book especially valuable for studying biblical lament and the theology of unanswered questions.

---

## 2. Habakkuk and the Problem of Evil

Habakkuk is one of the Old Testament's most significant books for the study of the **problem of evil**.

The prophet recognizes:

1. Judah is sinful.
2. God is holy.
3. God is sovereign.
4. Babylon is wicked.
5. God intends to use Babylon against Judah.
6. Babylon will itself be judged.

This shows that God's sovereignty does not mean moral approval of the actions of human agents.

---

## 3. Habakkuk 2:4 Is Foundational in the New Testament

Habakkuk 2:4 is quoted in three major New Testament passages.

### Romans 1:17

Paul uses the verse in the context of the revelation of God's righteousness through faith.

### Galatians 3:11

Paul uses it to emphasize that righteousness before God is not established through the law.

### Hebrews 10:38

The verse is connected with perseverance and faithful endurance.

Thus one short statement from Habakkuk becomes deeply significant for New Testament theology.

---

## 4. Faith and Faithfulness

The Hebrew word in Habakkuk 2:4 is commonly transliterated **'emunah**.

It can carry ideas such as:

* faith
* faithfulness
* steadfastness
* firmness
* reliability

This explains why translations may differ slightly.

The verse therefore presents a person who does not live like the proud person in the preceding context.

The righteous person lives in **trusting dependence upon God and steadfast faithfulness to Him**.

---

## 5. Habakkuk's Watchtower

Habakkuk 2:1 presents a striking image:

> "I will take my stand at my watchpost."

The watchman waits and watches for what God will say.

This illustrates:

**Prayer + waiting + listening + faith.**

The prophet does not abandon the conversation because the first answer was difficult.

He waits for God.

---

## 6. Five Woes Against Babylon

Habakkuk 2:6–20 contains a series of "woes."

They expose the sins of arrogant imperial power:

* exploitation
* greed
* unjust security
* violence
* humiliation of others
* idolatry

The passage demonstrates that political and military power does not place a nation beyond God's moral authority.

---

## 7. The Meaning of "The Earth Will Be Filled"

Habakkuk 2:14 says:

> "For the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD as the waters cover the sea."

This contrasts sharply with Babylon's attempt to fill the world with its own glory.

Human empire seeks:

**"Look at our greatness."**

God declares:

**"My glory will fill the earth."**

The passage therefore contrasts human ambition with divine glory.

---

## 8. Chapter 3 Is a Prayer and Song

Habakkuk 3 has musical/liturgical features.

The chapter ends with:

> "To the choirmaster: with stringed instruments."

This indicates that the prayer was associated with worship.

The book therefore does not merely teach people how to **think about suffering**.

It teaches them how to **worship God during suffering**.

---

## 9. The Meaning of Shigionoth

Habakkuk 3:1 describes the prayer as being offered:

> "according to Shigionoth."

The exact meaning is uncertain.

It appears to be some type of musical or poetic designation, possibly indicating a particular style or form of song.

Because the term is obscure, interpretations should be held with appropriate caution.

---

## 10. Habakkuk and Jonah

There is an interesting contrast between **Jonah and Habakkuk**.

Jonah struggles with God's **mercy** toward Nineveh.

Habakkuk struggles with God's **judgment** through Babylon.

Both prophets bring difficult questions to God.

Both demonstrate that God's ways can challenge human expectations.

Together they remind readers that:

**God's mercy and God's justice are both part of His character.**

---

## 11. Habakkuk and Nahum

Habakkuk and Nahum are also historically connected through the changing empires of the ancient Near East.

**Nahum** announces the fall of Assyria/Nineveh.

**Habakkuk** wrestles with the rise of Babylon after Assyria's decline.

The sequence illustrates the temporary nature of human empires.

One empire falls.

Another rises.

Yet God remains sovereign.

---

Habakkuk uses:

* dialogue
* lament
* rhetorical questions
* prophetic revelation
* poetic imagery
* repetition
* contrast
* "woe" sayings
* prayer
* worship
* historical-theological reflection

The book's structure itself communicates spiritual transformation.

It begins with **"How long?"**

It ends with **"Yet I will rejoice."**

---

## 13. Important Contrasts in Habakkuk

| Human Perspective             | God's Perspective           |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| "You do not hear."            | "I am doing a work."        |
| "Why tolerate injustice?"     | "Judgment is coming."       |
| Babylon seems unstoppable     | Babylon will be judged      |
| Circumstances appear hopeless | God's purposes continue     |
| The proud trust themselves    | The righteous live by faith |
| Crops may fail                | God remains worthy of joy   |
| Human strength disappears     | God becomes strength        |

---

## 14. The Theology of Waiting

Habakkuk teaches that waiting is not necessarily passive.

The prophet:

1. complains honestly
2. watches
3. listens
4. receives God's revelation
5. waits for God's appointed time
6. remembers God's works
7. worships

Biblical waiting is therefore an active posture of **trust and expectation**.

---

## 15. Habakkuk and Christian Worship

Habakkuk 3:17–19 is particularly useful for teaching about worship during difficult seasons.

The prophet does not say:

**"I will rejoice because everything is going well."**

Instead, he says essentially:

**Even if everything fails, I will rejoice in God.**

This teaches that Christian joy ultimately rests in **God Himself**, not merely in:

* financial prosperity
* health
* possessions
* success
* favorable circumstances
* predictable outcomes

---

## 16. The Central Message for Teachers

A helpful teaching sentence for the whole book is:

> **When we cannot understand what God is doing, we can still trust who God is.**

This captures the movement of Habakkuk without suggesting that every question receives an immediate explanation.

# Conclusion

The Book of Habakkuk is a profound journey from **confusion to confidence**.

Habakkuk begins by looking at the world and asking:

> **"How long?"**

He then listens to God and discovers that God's purposes are larger and more complex than he expected.

The prophet learns that:

* God is sovereign.
* God is holy.
* God is just.
* God sees injustice.
* God judges pride.
* God controls history.
* God's timing can require patience.
* The righteous are called to live by faith.
* Human empires are temporary.
* God Himself is the source of strength.
* Worship does not depend entirely upon circumstances.

The book does not end with every problem explained.

It ends with **faith**.

> **"Yet I will rejoice in the LORD; I will take joy in the God of my salvation. GOD, the Lord, is my strength." — Habakkuk 3:18–19**

### One-Sentence Summary

**Habakkuk teaches that when God's ways are difficult to understand and circumstances are difficult to endure, His people are called to wait faithfully, trust His sovereignty and justice, and rejoice in God Himself.**

### Key Message

**"The righteous shall live by his faith." — Habakkuk 2:4**

### Spiritual Journey of Habakkuk

**Question → Prayer → Waiting → Revelation → Faith → Worship**
