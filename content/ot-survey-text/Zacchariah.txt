# ZECHARIAH Survey

# Introduction

The Book of **Zechariah** is the eleventh book among the Twelve Minor Prophets in the traditional Christian arrangement of the Old Testament. It contains **14 chapters** and is one of the richest prophetic books in Scripture concerning **restoration, the coming Messiah, Jerusalem, the temple, God's kingdom, the nations, and the Day of the LORD**.

Zechariah ministered during the **post-exilic period**, when the Jewish people had returned from Babylon and were rebuilding Jerusalem and the temple. He was a contemporary of the prophet **Haggai**, and both prophets encouraged the returned community to complete the rebuilding of the temple.

Yet Zechariah's message goes far beyond the immediate rebuilding project. His visions and prophecies look forward to God's larger purposes for His people and ultimately to the coming **King, Shepherd, Branch, and Messiah**.

The book contains several passages that become especially significant in the New Testament:

* The coming King riding on a donkey — **Zechariah 9:9; Matthew 21:4–5; John 12:14–15**
* The thirty pieces of silver — **Zechariah 11:12–13; Matthew 26:14–15; 27:9–10**
* The pierced one — **Zechariah 12:10; John 19:37; Revelation 1:7**
* The striking of the Shepherd — **Zechariah 13:7; Matthew 26:31; Mark 14:27**
* The Branch — **Zechariah 3:8; 6:12–13**
* The future reign of the LORD — **Zechariah 14:9**

Zechariah therefore stands at an important point between **Old Testament restoration hope and New Testament Messianic fulfillment**.

## Relevance for Christians Today

Zechariah teaches that:

* God remembers His covenant promises.
* God's people must return to Him in repentance.
* God's work is accomplished through His Spirit rather than merely human strength.
* God cares about holiness and justice, not religious activity alone.
* God is present with His people.
* The Messiah is both King and Shepherd.
* God's kingdom ultimately extends beyond Israel to the nations.
* Present difficulties do not cancel God's future purposes.
* The LORD will ultimately establish His universal reign.

# Name of the Book

The Hebrew name is:

**זְכַרְיָה — Zekharyah / Zechariah**

The name is derived from Hebrew elements associated with:

* **זכר (zākar)** — to remember
* **יה (Yah)** — a shortened form of Yahweh

The name therefore means:

> **“YHWH remembers”**
> or
> **“The LORD has remembered.”**

This meaning is particularly appropriate to the message of the book.

The Jewish exiles had experienced judgment, destruction, and displacement. Zechariah reminded them that God's discipline did not mean that God had abandoned His covenant purposes.

The opening call captures this message:

> “Return to me, says the LORD of hosts, and I will return to you.”
> — **Zechariah 1:3**

The name Zechariah itself points toward the theological message of the book: **God remembers His promises and acts according to them.**

# Author of the Book

## Traditional Author

The book identifies its prophet as:

**Zechariah son of Berechiah, son of Iddo.**

> “In the eighth month, in the second year of Darius, the word of the LORD came to the prophet Zechariah the son of Berechiah, son of Iddo...”
> — **Zechariah 1:1**

He is also mentioned in **Ezra 5:1** and **Ezra 6:14** alongside Haggai.

## Zechariah's Historical Role

Zechariah was a prophet during the period when the Jewish community was rebuilding the temple after the Babylonian exile.

His ministry overlapped with:

* Haggai
* Zerubbabel
* Joshua/Jeshua the high priest
* the reign of Darius I of Persia

Haggai emphasized the immediate need to rebuild the temple, while Zechariah combined that message with a much broader vision of God's future purposes.

## Scholarly Discussion

Chapters **1–8** and **9–14** differ significantly in literary style.

Some modern scholars therefore propose that the later chapters were written or edited at a different period. Others maintain that the whole book belongs to the prophet Zechariah, understanding the stylistic differences as a result of different prophetic settings, genres, and purposes.

The traditional Christian and Jewish canonical reading treats the book as a unified work associated with Zechariah.

For Christian study, it is useful to recognize the scholarly discussion while observing that the canonical book presents a coherent theological movement from:

**repentance → restoration → holiness → Messiah → kingdom → final restoration.**

# Date Written

## Approximate Date

The earliest material in Zechariah dates to approximately:

**520–518 BC**

The book contains unusually precise chronological references.

### Major Dates

| Date   | Event                       |
| ------ | --------------------------- |
| 520 BC | Zechariah's ministry begins |
| 520 BC | First prophetic message     |
| 519 BC | Eight night visions         |
| 518 BC | Teaching concerning fasting |
| 516 BC | Second Temple completed     |

Zechariah 1:1 places the beginning of his ministry in the **second year of Darius**.

Zechariah 1:7 dates the night visions to the **24th day of the 11th month**, in the second year of Darius.

Zechariah 7:1 dates another message to the **fourth year of Darius**, approximately 518 BC.

## Historical Timeline

* **722 BC** — Assyria destroys the northern kingdom of Israel.
* **586 BC** — Babylon destroys Jerusalem and Solomon's temple.
* **539 BC** — Babylon falls to Persia.
* **538 BC** — Cyrus permits Jewish exiles to return.
* **c. 538 BC** — Temple rebuilding begins.
* **520 BC** — Haggai and Zechariah encourage the people.
* **516 BC** — Second Temple completed.
* **Later Persian period** — Further restoration and development of the Jewish community.

# Audience / To Whom Was It Written

Zechariah primarily addressed the **post-exilic Jewish community in Judah and Jerusalem**.

His immediate audience included:

* The returned Jewish community
* Temple builders
* Priests
* Zerubbabel, governor of Judah
* Joshua/Jeshua, the high priest
* Jerusalem's inhabitants

The audience was living in a difficult period.

They had returned from exile, but Jerusalem had not yet experienced the complete restoration promised by the prophets.

Zechariah therefore encouraged them not merely to rebuild a building, but to understand their work within God's larger redemptive plan.

# Historical and Geographical Setting

Zechariah ministered during the **Persian period**.

The Babylonian Empire had fallen, and Judah was now under Persian authority.

The Jewish people were permitted to return to their homeland, but the restoration was incomplete.

Jerusalem remained relatively weak, the temple was unfinished, and the community faced discouragement.

## Political Setting

The Persian king during Zechariah's ministry was:

**Darius I (Darius the Great)**

He ruled the Persian Empire from approximately 522–486 BC.

Judah existed as a Persian province commonly associated with **Yehud**.

## Religious Setting

The people had returned from exile, but spiritual restoration remained necessary.

Zechariah repeatedly emphasized:

* Repentance
* Obedience
* Justice
* Holiness
* Worship
* God's presence
* Restoration

The rebuilding of the temple was therefore both a physical and spiritual symbol of restoration.

# Purpose of the Book

Zechariah was written to accomplish several related purposes.

## 1. Call God's People to Repentance

> “Return to me, says the LORD of hosts, and I will return to you.”
> — **Zechariah 1:3**

## 2. Encourage the Rebuilding of the Temple

The returned community needed encouragement to continue God's work.

## 3. Assure God's Presence

God had not abandoned Jerusalem.

## 4. Encourage Zerubbabel and Joshua

The governor and high priest represented important aspects of the restored community.

## 5. Reveal God's Future Purposes

Zechariah's visions move beyond the immediate historical situation toward:

* Messiah
* Jerusalem's restoration
* judgment of nations
* God's kingdom
* final holiness
* universal worship

## 6. Teach True Covenant Faithfulness

Zechariah makes clear that fasting and religious ceremonies are not substitutes for justice, mercy, and obedience.

See **Zechariah 7:9–10**.

# Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

## Chapter 1 – Return to the LORD and the First Visions

Zechariah begins by calling the people to repentance.

> “Return to me... and I will return to you.”
> — **Zechariah 1:3**

He reminds them not to repeat the sins of their ancestors.

### First Vision: Horsemen Among the Myrtle Trees

Zechariah sees horsemen among myrtle trees.

The angel reports that the earth is relatively quiet, but God declares His concern for Jerusalem.

God promises:

* Compassion for Jerusalem
* Restoration
* Rebuilding
* Prosperity
* Return to Jerusalem

### Second Vision: Four Horns and Four Craftsmen

Four horns represent powers that scattered Judah, Jerusalem, and Israel.

Four craftsmen appear to terrify and overthrow those oppressive powers.

### Spiritual Lessons

* God calls His people to repentance.
* God has not forgotten Jerusalem.
* Earthly powers are accountable to God.
* God's purposes continue despite historical upheaval.

---

## Chapter 2 – The Measuring Line

Zechariah sees a man with a measuring line preparing to measure Jerusalem.

The message is that Jerusalem will become so populated that it cannot be contained by ordinary walls.

God promises:

> “I will be to her a wall of fire all around.”

The chapter also calls God's people to leave the lands of exile and return.

### Major Theme

**God Himself will dwell among His people.**

### Spiritual Lessons

* God's presence is greater than human defenses.
* Restoration includes God's presence.
* God's purposes extend beyond Israel.
* The nations are included in God's future work.

---

## Chapter 3 – Joshua the High Priest and the Branch

Joshua the high priest stands before the angel of the LORD.

Satan stands to accuse him.

Joshua is clothed in filthy garments, symbolizing impurity.

God commands that the filthy garments be removed and gives Joshua clean garments.

This illustrates God's gracious cleansing.

### The Branch

God introduces the figure called:

**“the Branch.”**

> “Behold, I will bring my servant the Branch.”
> — **Zechariah 3:8**

The Branch becomes one of the important Messianic titles in Zechariah.

### Spiritual Lessons

* God can remove guilt and impurity.
* God's grace overcomes accusation.
* Spiritual restoration involves holiness.
* The Messiah is connected with God's restoration purposes.

---

## Chapter 4 – The Lampstand and the Two Olive Trees

Zechariah sees a golden lampstand supplied by two olive trees.

The central message is given to Zerubbabel:

> “Not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit.”

### Meaning

The rebuilding of the temple will not ultimately succeed because of human strength.

It will succeed because of God's Spirit.

### Zerubbabel

Zerubbabel is told that the mountain before him will become a plain.

The vision assures him that the temple project will be completed.

### Two Olive Trees

The two olive trees are described as:

> “the two anointed ones who stand by the Lord of the whole earth.”
> — **Zechariah 4:14**

They are commonly understood in the immediate context as representing **Joshua the priest and Zerubbabel the governor**, though interpretations vary.

### Spiritual Lessons

* God's work depends upon God's Spirit.
* Small beginnings should not be despised.
* God supplies what is necessary for His purposes.
* Spiritual work should not be measured solely by human strength.

---

## Chapter 5 – The Flying Scroll and the Woman in the Basket

### The Flying Scroll

Zechariah sees a huge flying scroll.

It represents God's judgment against:

* Thieves
* Those who swear falsely
* Persistent wickedness

### The Woman in the Basket

Zechariah then sees an ephah/basket containing a woman representing **wickedness**.

She is carried away to Shinar.

Shinar recalls the region associated with ancient Babylon.

### Spiritual Lessons

* God judges persistent sin.
* Wickedness cannot remain permanently among God's restored people.
* Restoration includes cleansing.

---

## Chapter 6 – Four Chariots and the Crowning of Joshua

### Four Chariots

Four chariots emerge from between two mountains.

They represent heavenly agents going throughout the earth.

The vision emphasizes God's sovereign activity among the nations.

### The Crowning of Joshua

Zechariah is commanded to make a crown and place it upon Joshua the high priest.

Then comes the significant prophecy:

> “Behold, the man whose name is the Branch.”

— **Zechariah 6:12**

The Branch will:

* Branch out
* Build the temple of the LORD
* Bear royal glory
* Sit and rule
* Serve as priest on His throne

### Christological Importance

This is one of the most significant passages in Zechariah.

It combines **priestly and royal imagery** in the figure of the Branch.

This points toward the biblical expectation of a Messiah who brings together God's kingly and priestly purposes.

### Spiritual Lessons

* God directs history.
* The temple points beyond itself to God's larger purposes.
* God's promised ruler will bring lasting restoration.

---

## Chapter 7 – Fasting and Obedience

People ask whether they should continue fasting.

God responds by exposing the problem with empty religious ritual.

The question is not simply:

**“Did you fast?”**

but:

**“Did you obey God?”**

God reminds them that He had commanded their ancestors to practice:

* Justice
* Mercy
* Compassion
* Care for the vulnerable

See **Zechariah 7:9–10**.

### Spiritual Lessons

* Religious rituals cannot replace obedience.
* God cares about justice and mercy.
* Worship must affect how people treat others.
* Spiritual disciplines must come from genuine devotion.

---

## Chapter 8 – Jerusalem Restored

God promises that He will return to Zion and dwell in Jerusalem.

Jerusalem is pictured as a city filled with:

* Old men
* Old women
* Children
* Families
* Joy
* Peace

The city once associated with judgment will become associated with blessing.

### Fasting Becomes Feasting

God promises to transform fasting into joyful celebration.

### The Nations

One of the most remarkable statements appears in **Zechariah 8:23**.

People from many nations will seek the LORD and recognize that God is with His people.

### Spiritual Lessons

* God can transform mourning into joy.
* Restoration affects the whole community.
* God's purposes ultimately extend to the nations.

---

## Chapter 9 – The Coming King

Chapter 9 begins with judgment against surrounding nations.

The focus eventually turns toward Zion.

### The Coming King

One of Zechariah's most famous Messianic prophecies appears:

> “Behold, your king is coming to you; righteous and having salvation, humble and mounted on a donkey.”

— **Zechariah 9:9**

This prophecy is explicitly connected to Jesus' triumphal entry in:

* **Matthew 21:4–5**
* **John 12:14–15**

### The King's Rule

Verse 10 describes worldwide dominion and peace.

The King is therefore more than merely a local political ruler.

### Spiritual Lessons

* God's King is righteous.
* God's salvation is associated with humility.
* God's kingdom extends beyond Israel.
* True kingship is not defined merely by military power.

---

## Chapter 10 – Restoration of God's People

The chapter contrasts false shepherds and idols with the LORD's care for His people.

God promises to strengthen Judah and restore His people.

Ephraim is also included in restoration imagery.

### Major Themes

* God as Shepherd
* Restoration
* Strength
* Return
* God's faithfulness

### Spiritual Lesson

God is able to gather and strengthen people who have been scattered.

---

## Chapter 11 – The Shepherd and Thirty Pieces of Silver

This chapter contains difficult symbolic material involving shepherds.

The prophet portrays the rejection of God's shepherding care.

### Thirty Pieces of Silver

Zechariah says:

> “So they weighed out as my wages thirty pieces of silver.”

— **Zechariah 11:12**

The silver is thrown into the house of the LORD to the potter.

### New Testament Connection

Matthew 26:14–16 records Judas receiving **thirty pieces of silver** for betraying Jesus.

Matthew 27:9–10 connects the purchase and the field/potter imagery with prophetic Scripture.

### Important Interpretive Note

Matthew 27:9 attributes the quotation to **Jeremiah**, while much of the verbal correspondence is clearly associated with **Zechariah 11:12–13**, with possible thematic or verbal connections to Jeremiah.

This is an important example of why New Testament quotations should be studied carefully rather than reduced to simplistic one-to-one quotation formulas.

### Spiritual Lesson

The chapter presents the tragedy of rejecting God's appointed shepherd.

---

## Chapter 12 – Jerusalem and the Pierced One

God promises to defend Jerusalem.

The nations gather against Jerusalem, but God acts on behalf of His people.

### The Pierced One

One of the most important verses in the chapter says:

> “They will look on me, on him whom they have pierced.”

— **Zechariah 12:10**

The passage describes mourning and repentance.

### New Testament Connection

John 19:37 explicitly connects this passage with Jesus' crucifixion:

> “They will look on him whom they have pierced.”

See also:

* **Revelation 1:7**

### Spiritual Lessons

* God's salvation involves repentance.
* Mourning can become the beginning of spiritual restoration.
* The pierced figure has major Messianic significance.
* God's grace produces genuine repentance.

---

## Chapter 13 – Cleansing and the Struck Shepherd

A fountain is opened for cleansing from sin and impurity.

The chapter describes the removal of idolatry and false prophecy.

### The Struck Shepherd

God declares:

> “Strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered.”

— **Zechariah 13:7**

### New Testament Connection

Jesus applies this prophecy to His arrest and the scattering of His disciples.

See:

* **Matthew 26:31**
* **Mark 14:27**

### Spiritual Lessons

* God provides cleansing from sin.
* False worship must be removed.
* God's Shepherd experiences suffering.
* The scattering of God's people does not mean the end of God's purposes.

---

## Chapter 14 – The Day of the LORD

The final chapter contains one of the most dramatic eschatological passages in the Old Testament.

Jerusalem experiences conflict, but the LORD intervenes.

### Mount of Olives

The Mount of Olives is described as being split in two.

### Living Waters

Living waters flow from Jerusalem.

> “Living waters shall flow out from Jerusalem.”

— **Zechariah 14:8**

### The LORD Becomes King

The climax is:

> “And the LORD will be king over all the earth.”

— **Zechariah 14:9**

### Universal Worship

The nations are pictured coming to worship the King.

The chapter also describes holiness becoming pervasive throughout Jerusalem and the worship of the LORD.

### Spiritual Lessons

* God's kingdom is universal.
* God ultimately defeats opposition.
* God's presence brings transformation.
* The final goal of redemption is holiness and worship.

# Summary of the Book

The Book of Zechariah is a message of **repentance, encouragement, restoration, holiness, and future hope**.

The prophet ministered to a discouraged post-exilic community that was rebuilding the temple and Jerusalem.

The first part of the book contains a series of symbolic visions showing that God is active behind the scenes of history.

The second major section deals with:

* Fasting
* Justice
* Restoration
* Jerusalem
* The nations
* The coming King
* The Shepherd
* The pierced one
* The Day of the LORD

The book repeatedly moves from the immediate historical situation to God's larger redemptive purposes.

Its central message can be summarized:

> **God remembers His people, calls them to return to Him, promises His presence, and will ultimately establish His righteous kingdom.**

# Additional Important Information

## 1. Literary Structure

Zechariah contains several different literary forms.

### Prophetic Sermon

**1:1–6**

### Symbolic Night Visions

**1:7–6:8**

### Symbolic Action

**6:9–15**

### Prophetic Teaching

**7–8**

### Oracles

**9–14**

This mixture makes Zechariah one of the most visually rich books in the Old Testament.

---

## 2. The Eight Night Visions

The visions in chapters 1–6 are:

1. **Horsemen among the myrtle trees** — 1:7–17
2. **Four horns and four craftsmen** — 1:18–21
3. **Man with the measuring line** — 2:1–13
4. **Joshua the high priest** — 3:1–10
5. **Lampstand and two olive trees** — 4:1–14
6. **Flying scroll** — 5:1–4
7. **Woman in the basket/ephah** — 5:5–11
8. **Four chariots** — 6:1–8

These visions should not be treated as random dreams.

They form a theological sequence concerning:

* God's concern for Jerusalem
* Judgment of oppressors
* Protection
* Cleansing
* God's Spirit
* Removal of wickedness
* Divine judgment
* God's sovereignty over nations

---

## 3. The Branch

The Hebrew word is:

**צֶמַח — tsemach**

Meaning:

**“Branch,” “sprout,” or “shoot.”**

It occurs prominently in:

* **Zechariah 3:8**
* **Zechariah 6:12**

The Branch theme connects with broader Old Testament Messianic imagery such as:

* **Isaiah 11:1**
* **Jeremiah 23:5**
* **Jeremiah 33:15**

The imagery points toward a divinely appointed ruler associated with restoration.

---

## 4. Joshua and Zerubbabel

Two important leaders appear in Zechariah:

### Joshua

The high priest.

He represents the restored priesthood.

### Zerubbabel

The governor of Judah.

He represents the restored Davidic/governing leadership.

Their presence together is important because Zechariah repeatedly connects:

**priesthood + kingship + temple + God's future restoration.**

---

## 5. Zechariah and Haggai

Zechariah and Haggai were contemporaries.

Both encouraged the Jewish community to rebuild the temple.

### Haggai

Haggai strongly emphasized:

* Priorities
* Rebuilding
* Obedience
* God's presence

### Zechariah

Zechariah expanded the vision toward:

* Restoration
* Messiah
* Jerusalem
* Nations
* God's kingdom
* Future holiness

Together, the two books provide complementary perspectives on the post-exilic restoration.

## 7. Important Interpretive Caution

Zechariah contains highly symbolic material.

Not every image should automatically be converted into a literal prediction of a modern event.

Sound interpretation should consider:

1. The immediate historical context
2. The Old Testament background
3. The literary genre
4. The symbolism used by the prophet
5. The New Testament's own use of the passage
6. The distinction between historical restoration and eschatological expectation

---

## 8. The Mount of Olives

Zechariah 14:4 describes the Mount of Olives in an eschatological vision.

The New Testament also places important events involving Jesus on or near the Mount of Olives:

* **Matthew 24:3**
* **Matthew 26:30**
* **Luke 22:39**
* **Acts 1:9–12**

Acts 1:12 specifically identifies the disciples' location in relation to the Mount of Olives.

However, the New Testament does not explicitly state that every detail of Zechariah 14 has already been fulfilled in Acts 1.

The safest approach is to recognize the **strong thematic and geographical connection** while preserving the eschatological character of Zechariah 14.

---

## 9. The Meaning of “In That Day”

The expression **“in that day”** occurs repeatedly in Zechariah, especially in chapters 12–14.

It signals prophetic attention toward God's decisive intervention.

Depending on the passage, it may encompass:

* Historical events
* Messianic fulfillment
* Final judgment
* Restoration
* God's kingdom

This is why Zechariah's later chapters are important for biblical eschatology.

## 11. A Major Message for Bible Teachers

A useful teaching framework for Zechariah is:

**Return → Rebuild → Receive God's Spirit → Be Cleansed → Look to the Branch → Welcome the King → Trust the Shepherd → Await the Kingdom**

This captures the movement of the book from the post-exilic community's immediate needs toward God's ultimate redemptive purposes.

# Conclusion

The Book of Zechariah is a powerful prophetic message of **hope after judgment**.

The people had returned from exile, but their restoration was incomplete. Zechariah reminded them that God's purposes were much greater than merely rebuilding a physical temple.

Through visions, symbols, prophetic oracles, and Messianic promises, Zechariah reveals a God who:

* Calls His people to return
* Cleanses the guilty
* Dwells among His people
* Works through His Spirit
* Restores Jerusalem
* Judges wickedness
* Promises the Branch
* Sends the humble King
* Reveals the suffering Shepherd
* Speaks of the pierced one
* Extends salvation toward the nations
* Establishes His universal kingdom

For Christians, Zechariah is especially significant because several of its prophecies are explicitly connected by the New Testament with **Jesus Christ**.

The humble King of **Zechariah 9:9** enters Jerusalem on a donkey.

The pierced one of **Zechariah 12:10** is connected with Christ's crucifixion.

The struck Shepherd of **Zechariah 13:7** is applied by Jesus to His arrest and the scattering of His disciples.

And the final vision points toward the day when:

> “The LORD will be king over all the earth.”

— **Zechariah 14:9**

The book therefore calls God's people to live faithfully in the present while looking confidently toward the future fulfillment of God's promises.

**Central Message of Zechariah:**

> **Return to the LORD, trust His Spirit, live in holiness, and look to the coming King who will ultimately establish God's righteous kingdom.**

Zechariah is particularly valuable for a **Messianic-prophecy study series**, because it provides many direct bridges from the Old Testament into the Gospel accounts. The next book in the traditional sequence is **Malachi**, the final book of the Old Testament and the final Minor Prophet.
