# Nahum Survey

---

# Introduction

The **Book of Nahum** is the seventh book among the **Twelve Minor Prophets** in the Old Testament. It contains **three chapters** and focuses primarily on the announced judgment and destruction of **Nineveh**, the capital of the Assyrian Empire.

Nahum's message is striking when read alongside the Book of **Jonah**. Jonah records Nineveh's repentance and God's mercy toward the city. Nahum, written generations later, announces Nineveh's final judgment because Assyria had returned to violence, oppression, cruelty, and arrogance.

The book therefore addresses an important biblical question:

> **Will God ultimately hold violent and oppressive powers accountable?**

Nahum answers yes.

At the same time, Nahum begins by affirming God's character:

> **"The LORD is slow to anger and great in power, and the LORD will by no means clear the guilty."**
> — Nahum 1:3

Nahum matters to Christians today because it demonstrates that:

* God is sovereign over nations and empires.
* God's patience should never be mistaken for indifference.
* Evil and oppression will ultimately face divine judgment.
* God is a refuge for those who trust Him.
* Human empires are temporary, but God's rule is enduring.
* God's justice and God's goodness must be understood together.

---

# Name of the Book

The Hebrew name is:

**נַחוּם — Naḥûm / Nahum**

### Meaning of the Name

The name **Nahum** is related to the Hebrew root:

**נחם — n-ḥ-m**

The name is generally understood to mean:

> **"Comfort"**
> or
> **"Comforter"**

The name is particularly meaningful because Nahum's prophecy announces judgment upon Assyria, an empire that had inflicted enormous suffering on surrounding nations.

For Judah, the fall of Assyria could therefore represent **comfort through the removal of an oppressor**.

### Why is the Book Called Nahum?

The book begins:

> **"An oracle concerning Nineveh. The book of the vision of Nahum of Elkosh."**
> — Nahum 1:1

It is named after the prophet through whom this oracle was delivered.

---

# Author of the Book

## Traditional Author

The prophet is identified as:

> **Nahum of Elkosh**

> **Nahum 1:1**

Very little is known about Nahum's personal life.

## Internal Evidence

The book identifies Nahum as:

* A prophet
* The recipient of a divine vision
* A person associated with **Elkosh**
* The speaker of God's judgment against Nineveh

The book is highly poetic and contains sophisticated prophetic imagery.

## External Historical Evidence

The Book of Nahum became part of the collection of the **Twelve Minor Prophets** in the Hebrew Scriptures.

There is no extensive independent historical biography of Nahum preserved outside the biblical text.

Later Jewish and Christian traditions attempted to identify the location of Elkosh, but there is no universally accepted identification.

## Scholarly Opinions

The authorship of the book is generally associated with the historical prophet Nahum.

The primary scholarly questions concern:

* The precise location of Elkosh
* The exact date of the prophecy
* The literary development of certain sections
* The historical and poetic relationship between the prophecy and the fall of Nineveh

The canonical book itself presents the message as a prophetic **vision concerning Nineveh**.

---

# Date Written

## Approximate Date

Nahum was probably written sometime between:

**c. 663 BC and 612 BC**

The narrower date is based on historical references within the prophecy.

### The Lower Boundary: The Fall of Thebes

Nahum 3:8 refers to **No-amon**, generally identified with **Thebes in Egypt**, as an example of a city that had fallen.

The Assyrians captured Thebes around:

**663 BC**

Therefore, Nahum likely comes **after 663 BC**.

### The Upper Boundary: Fall of Nineveh

Nineveh fell to a coalition of the **Babylonians and Medes** in:

**612 BC**

Nahum predicts Nineveh's destruction, so the prophecy must predate or be closely associated with that event.

### Approximate Range

> **663–612 BC**

Many scholars place Nahum toward the **middle or latter part of the 7th century BC**, before Nineveh's fall.

# Historical Background

## The Assyrian Empire

Assyria was one of the most powerful empires of the ancient Near East.

Its territory expanded across:

* Mesopotamia
* Syria
* The Levant
* Parts of Egypt
* Surrounding regions

Assyria was known for military expansion, tribute collection, deportation of conquered peoples, and imperial administration.

## Assyria and Israel

Assyria destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel.

> **2 Kings 17:5–6**

Samaria fell in:

**722 BC**

Many Israelites were deported.

## Assyria and Judah

Judah also experienced severe Assyrian pressure.

During the reign of **Hezekiah**, Sennacherib invaded Judah.

> **2 Kings 18–19**
>
> **Isaiah 36–37**

Jerusalem was threatened, but the city was not captured during that campaign.

## Why Nahum Matters Historically

By Nahum's time, Assyria had become a symbol of:

* Imperial violence
* Military oppression
* Pride
* Cruelty
* Exploitation

Nahum announces that even this apparently invincible empire would eventually fall.

---

# Audience / To Whom Was It Written

## Primary Audience

Nahum's immediate audience was likely the people of **Judah**.

The prophecy announces judgment against Nineveh and therefore has an important message for people living under Assyrian domination.

## Historical Audience

The book would have reminded Judah that:

> **Assyria's power was not ultimate.**

The LORD remained sovereign.

## Spiritual Purpose

Nahum encourages God's people to:

* Trust God's justice
* Remember God's sovereignty
* Refuse to fear human empires
* Take refuge in the LORD
* Understand that evil will not continue forever

## Relevance for Modern Believers

Nahum does not authorize Christians to seek personal revenge.

Instead, it teaches that:

> **Final justice belongs to God.**

Compare:

* **Deuteronomy 32:35**
* **Romans 12:19**

---

# Historical and Geographical Setting

## Political Background

Nahum lived during the period when Assyria was still a major international power.

However, Assyria's dominance was weakening.

Several developments contributed to its eventual collapse:

* Internal instability
* Political pressure
* The rise of Babylon
* The rise of the Medes
* Military opposition from surrounding peoples

Nineveh eventually fell in **612 BC**.

## Cultural Setting

The Assyrian Empire was highly organized and militarized.

Its rulers used monumental art and inscriptions to communicate imperial power and military victories.

Nahum's imagery intentionally contrasts this imperial self-confidence with the sovereignty of the LORD.

# Key Verses

## Nahum 1:2

**Verse Text:**

> "The LORD is a jealous and avenging God; the LORD is avenging and wrathful..."

**Explanation:**
God's judgment against evil is part of His holy character.

**Significance:**
The verse establishes that divine judgment is not uncontrolled human-style anger. It is connected with God's holiness and justice.

---

## Nahum 1:3

**Verse Text:**

> "The LORD is slow to anger and great in power, and the LORD will by no means clear the guilty."

**Explanation:**
God is patient but does not ignore guilt forever.

**Significance:**
This verse balances God's patience with His justice.

It also echoes:

> **Exodus 34:6–7**

---

## Nahum 1:7

**Verse Text:**

> "The LORD is good, a stronghold in the day of trouble; he knows those who take refuge in him."

**Explanation:**
In the middle of a judgment oracle, Nahum gives a beautiful statement about God's goodness and protection.

**Significance:**
This is one of the most important verses for personal devotion and pastoral teaching in Nahum.

---

## Nahum 1:15

**Verse Text:**

> "Behold, upon the mountains, the feet of him who brings good news..."

**Explanation:**
The image is of a messenger announcing deliverance.

**Significance:**
It has a strong connection with **Isaiah 52:7**, and its language emphasizes the end of Assyrian oppression.

---

## Nahum 2:13

**Verse Text:**

> "Behold, I am against you, declares the LORD of hosts..."

**Explanation:**
The LORD Himself declares judgment against Nineveh.

**Significance:**
Nineveh's real opponent is not merely another human empire but the sovereign God.

---

## Nahum 3:1

**Verse Text:**

> "Woe to the bloody city, all full of lies and plunder—no end to the prey!"

**Explanation:**
Nineveh's violence, deception, and exploitation are identified as reasons for judgment.

**Significance:**
The book makes clear that Nineveh is judged because of concrete moral evil.

---

## Nahum 3:19

**Verse Text:**

> "There is no easing your hurt; your wound is grievous."

**Explanation:**
The prophecy concludes with the irreversible nature of Nineveh's downfall.

**Significance:**
The mighty Assyrian empire is shown to be unable to save itself.

# Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

## Chapter 1 – The LORD Is Sovereign and Just

### Main Events

Nahum begins by identifying the message as:

> "An oracle concerning Nineveh."

> **Nahum 1:1**

The opening verses describe God's character.

God is:

* Jealous
* Avenging
* Wrathful against evil
* Slow to anger
* Great in power
* Just

> **Nahum 1:2–3**

The natural world responds to His presence:

* Mountains quake
* Hills melt
* Earth trembles

> **Nahum 1:5–6**

But the prophet then gives a remarkable statement:

> **"The LORD is good, a stronghold in the day of trouble."**

> **Nahum 1:7**

The chapter turns toward judgment against Nineveh and relief for Judah.

> **Nahum 1:12–13**

### Important Teachings

1. God's patience does not mean He ignores evil.
2. God is powerful enough to judge nations.
3. God's goodness and judgment belong together.
4. God is a refuge for those who trust Him.

### Spiritual Lessons

* Trust God's justice rather than taking revenge.
* Remember God's character when evil appears powerful.
* God's people can find refuge in Him during trouble.
* Human power is temporary.

---

# Chapter 2 – The Fall of Nineveh

Chapter 2 gives a dramatic poetic description of Nineveh's downfall.

The language is vivid and military.

Images include:

* Soldiers
* Shields
* Chariots
* Rushing through streets
* Gates of rivers
* Palace collapse
* Looting
* Emptying of the city

> **Nahum 2:3–10**

The once-powerful city becomes:

> **"desolate! Desolation and ruin!"**

> **Nahum 2:10**

The chapter also uses the imagery of a lion's den.

> **Nahum 2:11–12**

Assyria had acted like a powerful predator toward other nations.

Now the LORD declares:

> **"Behold, I am against you"**

> **Nahum 2:13**

### Important Teachings

* Military power cannot guarantee permanence.
* God can overturn human empires.
* Violence eventually brings accountability.
* Pride can precede destruction.

### Spiritual Lessons

1. Do not place ultimate trust in human strength.
2. Power without righteousness is unstable.
3. God sees oppression even when it appears successful.

---

# Chapter 3 – Why Nineveh Must Fall

Chapter 3 gives the clearest explanation for Nineveh's judgment.

Nineveh is described as:

> "the bloody city"

> **Nahum 3:1**

Its sins include:

* Violence
* Lies
* Plunder
* Exploitation
* Prostitution imagery
* Sorcery
* Deception

> **Nahum 3:1–4**

God compares Nineveh's coming destruction to the destruction of **No-amon**, identified with Thebes in Egypt.

> **Nahum 3:8–10**

The point is clear:

If even a powerful city like Thebes could fall, Nineveh should not assume that it is invincible.

The chapter ends with Nineveh's leaders being unable to save the empire.

> **Nahum 3:18–19**

### Important Teachings

* God judges systemic violence.
* Deception and exploitation matter to God.
* No empire is beyond accountability.
* Human arrogance cannot defeat divine sovereignty.

### Spiritual Lessons

* God sees injustice.
* Evil should not be mistaken for permanence.
* Pride can blind people to approaching judgment.
* God's people can trust Him when oppressive powers appear dominant.

# Foreshadows / Types of Jesus Christ

Nahum does **not contain a direct messianic prophecy comparable to Micah 5:2 or Isaiah 53**.

However, several theological themes contribute to the broader biblical understanding of Christ.

## 1. The LORD as Refuge

> **Nahum 1:7**

The LORD is described as a stronghold for those who trust Him.

This theme finds rich fulfillment in the New Testament's teaching about security and refuge in Christ.

Compare:

* **John 10:27–29**
* **Romans 8:31–39**
* **Hebrews 6:18**

---

## 2. Good News of Deliverance

Nahum 1:15 says:

> "the feet of him who brings good news"

This language closely resembles:

> **Isaiah 52:7**

The immediate context concerns Judah's deliverance from Assyrian oppression.

Paul later uses the language of **Isaiah 52:7** in connection with Gospel proclamation:

> **Romans 10:15**

Therefore, Nahum 1:15 can be understood as part of the broader biblical theme of **good news bringing deliverance**, although the immediate prophecy is about the end of Assyrian oppression rather than a direct prediction of Jesus.

---

## 3. Divine Judgment and Final Justice

Nahum's presentation of God's judgment contributes to the larger biblical doctrine that God will ultimately judge evil.

The New Testament applies this theme to the final judgment:

* **Acts 17:31**
* **Romans 2:5–8**
* **Revelation 20:11–15**

Nahum therefore contributes to the biblical framework of **God's righteous judgment**, even though it is not itself a direct messianic prophecy.

# Summary of the Book

The Book of Nahum is a prophetic announcement of the downfall of **Nineveh**, the capital of Assyria.

The book begins not with Nineveh's military weakness but with **God's character**.

God is:

* Holy
* Powerful
* Patient
* Just
* Good
* A refuge for those who trust Him

> **Nahum 1:2–7**

The prophecy then describes the destruction of Nineveh.

The reason for judgment is clearly identified:

* Violence
* Bloodshed
* Lies
* Plunder
* Exploitation
* Arrogance

> **Nahum 3:1–7**

### Main Theological Message

> **God is patient and good, but He will ultimately hold persistent evil and oppressive power accountable.**

### Spiritual Takeaway

Believers can find encouragement in the fact that:

> **God sees injustice, God remains sovereign, and evil does not have the final word.**

# Additional Important Information

## 1. Jonah and Nahum: The Same Nineveh, Different Generations

One of the most important ways to understand Nahum is to compare it with Jonah.

### Jonah

Nineveh:

> **Repents**

> Jonah 3:5–10

God:

> **Shows mercy**

> Jonah 3:10

### Nahum

Nineveh:

> **Faces judgment**

> Nahum 1–3

This demonstrates an important biblical principle:

> **God's mercy does not mean that persistent evil will never be judged.**

The two books should not be viewed as contradictory.

---

## 2. The Character of God in Nahum 1:2–7

Nahum 1 contains a carefully balanced presentation.

### God is:

**Jealous** — He will not tolerate rivals or injustice.

**Avenging** — He acts against evil.

**Slow to anger** — He is patient.

**Great in power** — His patience is not weakness.

**Good** — His judgment is consistent with His goodness.

**A stronghold** — His people can take refuge in Him.

This balance is essential for sound biblical theology.

---

## 3. Nahum and Exodus 34

Nahum 1:3 echoes the language of:

> **Exodus 34:6–7**

The LORD is:

* Slow to anger
* Great in steadfast love
* Just
* Not treating guilt as insignificant

This shows that Nahum's message of judgment is rooted in the broader Old Testament revelation of God's character.

---

## 4. Nineveh as a Symbol of Imperial Pride

Nineveh was a real historical city.

But within biblical theology, its downfall also illustrates a recurring principle:

> **Human kingdoms that exalt themselves against God are temporary.**

Similar themes appear in:

* **Isaiah 10:5–19**
* **Daniel 4:28–37**
* **Daniel 5:17–31**
* **Revelation 18**

---

## 5. The Lion Imagery

Nahum 2:11–13 portrays Nineveh in imagery associated with lions.

The lion represented:

* Strength
* Power
* Predatory dominance

Assyria had preyed upon surrounding nations.

But the LORD says:

> **"Behold, I am against you."**

> **Nahum 2:13**

The predator becomes the judged.

---

## 6. The "Bloody City"

Nahum 3:1 calls Nineveh:

> **"the bloody city"**

This provides an important interpretive key.

The prophecy is not simply:

> "God dislikes Nineveh."

Rather, the book identifies concrete reasons for divine judgment.

The judgment is associated with:

* Bloodshed
* Lies
* Plunder
* Exploitation
* Deception

---

## 7. The Fall of Nineveh

Nineveh's destruction occurred around:

> **612 BC**

Its fall represented a major geopolitical turning point in the ancient Near East.

The Assyrian Empire had dominated the region for generations, but its capital eventually fell to invading forces.

This historical event provides the background for understanding the dramatic imagery in Nahum 2–3.

## 8. Is Nahum Relevant to Christians Today?

Yes, but it should be applied carefully.

Nahum should not be used to encourage Christians to celebrate the suffering of enemies or pursue personal vengeance.

Instead, it teaches:

### Trust

God remains sovereign.

### Patience

God's timing is not the same as ours.

### Justice

God takes evil seriously.

### Refuge

God cares for those who trust Him.

### Humility

Human power is temporary.

### Hope

Oppression does not have the final word.

## 9. Important Hebrew Concepts

### Nahum — נַחוּם

**Comfort / consolation**

The name itself fits the book's effect on God's oppressed people.

---

### Qanna — קַנָּא

Related to the concept of **jealousy/zeal**.

In Scripture, God's jealousy reflects His covenant commitment and exclusive claim over His people.

> **Nahum 1:2**

---

### Ma'oz — מָעוֹז

A Hebrew term meaning:

> **stronghold / fortress / refuge**

It is used conceptually in:

> **Nahum 1:7**

This emphasizes God's protective strength.

## 10. Literary Style

Nahum is highly poetic.

The prophet uses:

* Metaphor
* Personification
* Military imagery
* Lion imagery
* Flood imagery
* Fire imagery
* City imagery
* Irony
* Taunt language

The vivid style is designed to communicate the certainty and seriousness of Nineveh's downfall.

---

## 11. Acrostic Structure in Nahum 1

Nahum 1 contains evidence of an **alphabetic acrostic or partial acrostic structure** in the Hebrew text.

Scholars differ regarding the exact reconstruction and extent of the acrostic.

This literary feature may have contributed to the poetic organization of the opening section.

It is best treated as a literary observation rather than as a doctrine.

---

## 12. Important Teaching Connection: God's Justice and God's Goodness

One of the most important teaching opportunities in Nahum is the relationship between:

> **Nahum 1:3 — God's justice**

and

> **Nahum 1:7 — God's goodness**

The same God is both:

**just toward evil**

and

**a refuge for His people.**

This prevents two theological errors:

### Error 1

"God is loving, therefore He never judges."

Nahum rejects this.

### Error 2

"God's judgment means God is cruel."

Nahum also rejects this.

Instead:

> **God's holiness, justice, patience, goodness, and mercy must be understood together.**

---

# Conclusion

The Book of Nahum is a powerful testimony to the **sovereignty and justice of God**.

Nineveh appeared to be an unstoppable imperial power. It had conquered nations, destroyed Samaria, threatened Judah, and accumulated enormous wealth and influence.

Yet Nahum announces:

> **"Behold, I am against you, declares the LORD of hosts."**
> — **Nahum 2:13**

The message is not simply about the destruction of an ancient city. It reveals a larger biblical truth:

> **No human power is ultimate, and no persistent evil is beyond God's judgment.**

At the same time, Nahum gives believers a deeply comforting truth:

> **"The LORD is good, a stronghold in the day of trouble; he knows those who take refuge in him."**
> — **Nahum 1:7**

When read alongside Jonah, Nahum also teaches an important lesson about God's dealings with nations. God is willing to show mercy when people repent, but persistent violence and rebellion do not escape His justice.

For Bible teachers, pastors, and Christian readers, Nahum provides rich material for studying:

* God's holiness
* Divine justice
* God's patience
* God's goodness
* Human pride
* Political power
* Oppression
* Divine judgment
* God's protection
* Trust during difficult times

The book ultimately calls God's people to **trust the LORD rather than fear human powers**, to leave final vengeance in God's hands, and to remember that the God who judges evil is also the God who provides refuge.

**Nahum's message can therefore be summarized in one sentence:**

> **God is good and sovereign; He is a refuge for those who trust Him, and He will ultimately hold violent and oppressive evil accountable.**
