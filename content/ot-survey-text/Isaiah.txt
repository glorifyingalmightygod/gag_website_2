* **First of the Major Prophets.**
* The longest prophetic book after Psalms in terms of chapters.

The book explains:

* Why judgment came upon Israel and Judah.
* Why exile was necessary.

## Literal Meaning

> **"Yahweh is Salvation"**
>
> or

> **"The LORD Saves."**

This perfectly summarizes the message of Isaiah.

## Why the Book Is Called Isaiah

The book records the visions, prophecies, sermons, historical narratives, and messages received by the prophet Isaiah during the reigns of several Judean kings.

Unlike some prophetic books named for their audience or theme, this book bears the name of the prophet through whom God delivered His message.

---

# Author of the Book

## Traditional View

The traditional and historic Christian and Jewish view is that the prophet **Isaiah son of Amoz** wrote the book.

---

## Who Was Isaiah?

Isaiah was:

* Prophet
* Preacher
* Statesman
* Counselor to kings
* Poet
* Historian
* Theologian

He served during one of the most critical periods in Judah's history.

## Isaiah's Characteristics

| Characteristic        | Description                                                                               |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Deeply devoted to God | His vision of God's holiness shaped his entire ministry (Isaiah 6).                       |
| Courageous            | He boldly confronted kings and the nation with God's truth.                               |
| Compassionate         | He mourned the people's sin and proclaimed hope for the faithful remnant.                 |

---

## Internal Evidence

The book repeatedly identifies Isaiah as its author (Isaiah 1:1; 2:1; 13:1; 20:2; 37–39).

The historical narratives align with Isaiah's prophetic ministry during the reigns of Judah's kings.

---

## External Historical Evidence

Ancient Jewish tradition, early Christian writers, and the New Testament uniformly attribute the book to Isaiah.

Jesus and the apostles quoted Isaiah as authoritative Scripture (e.g., Matthew 3:3; Luke 4:17–21; John 12:38–41; Romans 10:16–21).

The discovery of the **Great Isaiah Scroll** among the Dead Sea Scrolls (dated around the second century BC) shows that Isaiah was already circulating as a single, unified book centuries before Christ.

---

## Scholarly Opinions

Many modern scholars propose:

* **Single-author view**: Isaiah wrote the entire book.
* **Multiple-author view**: Often called "Deutero-Isaiah" (chapters 40–55) and "Trito-Isaiah" (chapters 56–66), suggesting later prophetic contributors.

### Balanced Assessment

The multiple-author theory is based mainly on differences in style, historical focus, and references to future events (such as Cyrus by name in Isaiah 44–45).

However, both Jewish and Christian tradition have consistently treated Isaiah as one unified book. The New Testament quotes passages from different sections simply as the words of "Isaiah." From a conservative evangelical perspective, predictive prophecy and literary diversity adequately explain the book's features without requiring multiple authors.

# Historical and Geographical Setting

## Historical Context

Isaiah prophesied during the decline of the Northern Kingdom and the turbulent years of the Southern Kingdom.

The dominant world power was **Assyria**, whose military campaigns threatened the entire region. Judah faced constant pressure to form political alliances, but Isaiah repeatedly urged the nation to trust the Lord instead.

---

## Political Background

Isaiah ministered during the reigns of:

| King                | Reign         | Spiritual Condition                                                          |
| ------------------- | ------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Uzziah              | c. 792–740 BC | Prosperous but pride led to judgment.                                        |
| Jotham              | c. 750–735 BC | Generally faithful, but idolatry persisted among the people.                 |
| Ahaz                | c. 735–715 BC | Extremely wicked; trusted Assyria instead of God.                            |
| Hezekiah            | c. 715–686 BC | Godly reformer who trusted the Lord during the Assyrian crisis.              |
| (Possibly) Manasseh | c. 686–642 BC | Tradition associates Isaiah's martyrdom with this reign (cf. Hebrews 11:37). |

---

## Cultural Setting

Judah enjoyed periods of prosperity but became marked by:

* Social injustice.
* Exploitation of the poor.
* Materialism.
* Corrupt leadership.
* Empty religious rituals.
* Pride and self-reliance.

---

## Religious Condition

The nation maintained temple worship while embracing idolatry and injustice. Isaiah declared that God desired obedient hearts rather than empty sacrifices (Isaiah 1:11–17).

Isaiah was written to call God's people back to covenant faithfulness while revealing His long-term plan of redemption.

### Why the Book Was Written

* To expose Judah's sin.
* To warn of coming judgment.
* To proclaim hope for a faithful remnant.
* To reveal the coming Messiah.
* To promise future restoration and a new creation.

**The Holy One of Israel judges sin but graciously saves all who trust in Him through His promised Servant.**

### Spiritual Objective

* Inspire repentance.
* Build faith in God's sovereignty.
* Encourage perseverance.
* Point all generations to the Messiah.

### Major Themes and Goals

* God's holiness.
* Judgment and mercy.
* The remnant.
* Trust versus fear.
* The coming King.
* The Suffering Servant.
* Universal salvation.
* The future kingdom of God.

# Key Verses

* **Isaiah 6:8** – *"Here am I. Send me!"*
  **Explanation:** Isaiah's willing response to God's call models wholehearted service.

* **Isaiah 7:14** – *"Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel."*
  **Explanation:** A foundational Messianic prophecy fulfilled in Jesus Christ (Matthew 1:22–23).

* **Isaiah 9:6–7** – *"For to us a child is born..."*
  **Explanation:** Foretells the divine King whose reign will have no end.

* **Isaiah 40:31** – *"Those who wait for the LORD shall renew their strength."*
  **Explanation:** Encourages believers to trust God's sustaining power.

* **Isaiah 53:5** – *"He was pierced for our transgressions..."*
  **Explanation:** One of the clearest Old Testament prophecies of Christ's atoning death.

* **Isaiah 55:6–7** – *"Seek the LORD while He may be found..."*
  **Explanation:** A gracious invitation to repentance and mercy.

* **Isaiah 66:22** – *"For as the new heavens and the new earth..."*
  **Explanation:** Looks forward to God's eternal kingdom and the renewal of creation.

# Historical Background Before Chapter 1

Before studying Isaiah chapter by chapter, it is important to understand what was happening in Israel and Judah.

## Timeline

| Event                       | Approximate Date    |
| --------------------------- | ------------------- |
| David becomes king          | c. 1010 BC          |
| Solomon builds the Temple   | c. 966 BC           |
| Kingdom divided             | 931 BC              |
| Northern Kingdom (Israel)   | Capital: Samaria    |
| Southern Kingdom (Judah)    | Capital: Jerusalem  |

During Isaiah's ministry:

* Israel (Northern Kingdom) was almost destroyed.
* Judah still had opportunities to repent.
* Idolatry had become widespread.
* Wealthy people oppressed the poor.
* Temple worship continued, but many hearts were far from God.

God therefore raised Isaiah to proclaim both judgment and hope.

# Major Theme of Chapters 1–11

These opening chapters introduce the central message of the book:

* Judah's rebellion
* God's holiness
* The need for repentance
* The coming Messiah
* The future Kingdom of God

These chapters move from **condemnation** to **hope**, establishing themes that continue throughout Isaiah.

# Chapter 1 – God's Case Against Judah

## Main Events

* God calls heaven and earth as witnesses against His people.
* Judah is compared to rebellious children.
* Jerusalem is described as spiritually sick.
* God rejects empty sacrifices and hypocritical worship.
* The people are called to repentance.
* God promises cleansing for those who return.
* Judgment is announced for the rebellious.

## Key Persons

* Isaiah
* Judah
* Jerusalem
* The LORD

## Important Teachings

### 1. Religion without obedience is unacceptable.

God declares that sacrifices, festivals, and prayers are meaningless when accompanied by injustice (Isaiah 1:11–15).

### 2. God desires justice.

True worship includes:

* Defending the oppressed.
* Caring for orphans.
* Pleading the cause of widows.

### 3. God's grace is available.

Isaiah 1:18 offers one of Scripture's great invitations:

> "Though your sins are like scarlet, they shall be as white as snow."

Repentance brings forgiveness.

## Spiritual Lessons

* God examines the heart, not merely outward rituals.
* Sin affects an entire nation.
* God's mercy is greater than human failure.
* Repentance always remains God's invitation.

---

# Chapter 2 – The Mountain of the LORD

## Main Events

* Isaiah sees the future glory of Zion.
* Nations stream to Jerusalem to learn God's ways.
* Universal peace is foretold.
* Human pride is condemned.
* Idolatry is denounced.
* The Day of the LORD is introduced.

## Important Teachings

### Future Kingdom

Isaiah foresees a time when:

* Christ reigns from Zion.
* Nations seek God's wisdom.
* Wars cease.
* Peace fills the earth.

### Human Pride

People trust:

* Wealth
* Military power
* Idols

instead of God.

God promises to humble all pride.

## Messianic Significance

The prophecy anticipates Christ's universal reign and the future Kingdom of God (cf. Micah 4:1–4).

## Spiritual Lessons

* God's kingdom will ultimately prevail.
* Pride always precedes judgment.
* Peace is found only under God's righteous rule.

---

# Chapter 3 – Judgment on Judah's Leaders

## Main Events

* God removes Judah's leaders.
* Society falls into chaos.
* Inexperienced rulers emerge.
* Women of Jerusalem are rebuked for pride and luxury.
* National collapse is described.

## Important Teachings

Leadership matters.

When leaders reject God:

* Justice disappears.
* Society deteriorates.
* Families suffer.
* Nations decline.

## Spiritual Lessons

* God holds leaders accountable.
* Pride invites judgment.
* A nation cannot prosper without righteousness.

---

# Chapter 4 – The Branch of the LORD

## Main Events

* Judgment gives way to hope.
* A faithful remnant survives.
* Jerusalem is cleansed.
* The "Branch of the LORD" appears.
* God's presence protects His people.

## Messianic Significance

"The Branch" points to the coming Messiah (see also Jeremiah 23:5; Zechariah 3:8).

Jesus is the righteous Branch who brings cleansing and restoration.

## Spiritual Lessons

* God's judgment is never His final word.
* He always preserves a faithful remnant.
* Christ brings beauty from ruin.

---

# Chapter 5 – The Song of the Vineyard

## Main Events

* Israel is pictured as God's vineyard.
* Despite God's care, the vineyard produces only bad fruit.
* Six "Woes" pronounce judgment.
* Foreign nations are summoned as instruments of discipline.

## Important Teachings

### The Vineyard

God planted Israel expecting:

* Justice
* Holiness
* Faithfulness

Instead He found:

* Violence
* Oppression
* Corruption

### Six Woes

Isaiah condemns:

1. Greed
2. Drunkenness
3. Moral inversion ("calling evil good")
4. Pride
5. Corrupt justice
6. Self-sufficiency

These remain timeless warnings.

## Spiritual Lessons

* Privilege increases responsibility.
* God expects spiritual fruit.
* Moral compromise destroys societies.

# Chapter 6 – Isaiah's Call

This chapter is one of the most significant in the Old Testament.

## Main Events

* King Uzziah dies.
* Isaiah sees the LORD enthroned.
* Seraphim proclaim God's holiness.
* Isaiah confesses his sin.
* His lips are cleansed with a burning coal.
* God commissions Isaiah.
* Isaiah accepts his calling.

## Vision of God

Isaiah sees:

* God's throne.
* Heavenly worship.
* Seraphim crying:

> "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD Almighty."

This threefold declaration emphasizes God's perfect holiness.

## Isaiah's Response

Rather than comparing himself with others, Isaiah compares himself with God and cries:

> "Woe is me!"

True worship produces humility.

## God's Grace

A burning coal from the altar symbolizes:

* Atonement
* Forgiveness
* Cleansing

Only after cleansing does God send Isaiah.

## Famous Verse

> "Here am I! Send me."

(Isaiah 6:8)

## Spiritual Lessons

* A vision of God changes everything.
* Holiness reveals our sinfulness.
* God cleanses before He commissions.
* Availability matters more than ability.

# Chapter 7 – The Sign of Immanuel

## Historical Setting

King Ahaz fears invasion by Israel and Syria.

Instead of trusting God, he plans political alliances.

Isaiah urges him to trust the LORD.

## Main Events

* Ahaz refuses God's sign.
* God gives one anyway.
* The prophecy of Immanuel is announced.

## Key Prophecy

> "Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel."

(Isaiah 7:14)

### Immediate Context

The sign assured Judah of God's presence during the crisis.

### Ultimate Fulfillment

Matthew 1:22–23 identifies Jesus Christ as the ultimate fulfillment.

## Spiritual Lessons

* Faith trusts God's promises.
* Fear often leads to poor decisions.
* God remains faithful even when people doubt.

# Chapter 8 – Trust God, Not Fear

## Main Events

* Isaiah's son receives the symbolic name Maher-Shalal-Hash-Baz.
* Assyria is foretold as God's instrument of judgment.

(Isaiah 8:12–13)

## Messianic Reference

> "a stone that causes people to stumble."

The New Testament applies this imagery to Christ (Romans 9:33; 1 Peter 2:8).

## Spiritual Lessons

* Faith overcomes fear.
* God's Word is a sure foundation.
* Christ is either a cornerstone or a stumbling stone.

# Overview of This Section (Isaiah 12–22)

After announcing the coming Messiah in chapters 7–11, Isaiah shifts his attention to **God's dealings with the nations**. These chapters demonstrate that the LORD is not only the God of Judah but the sovereign King over all peoples and kingdoms.

### Major Themes

* God's sovereignty over all nations
* Judgment against pride and idolatry
* Hope for a faithful remnant
* The certainty of God's Word
* Trusting God instead of political alliances

---

# Chapter 12 – A Song of Praise

## Main Events

* Isaiah records a hymn of thanksgiving.
* God's anger is turned away.
* The redeemed rejoice in salvation.
* The people are called to proclaim God's greatness among the nations.

## Important Teachings

This chapter serves as a response to the promises of chapters 1–11. After judgment comes salvation.

### Key Verse

> **Isaiah 12:2**
> "Behold, God is my salvation; I will trust and not be afraid."

The Hebrew word for "salvation" (*yeshuah*) is closely related to the name **Jesus (Yeshua)**, highlighting the ultimate source of deliverance.

## Spiritual Lessons

* Salvation leads to worship.
* Gratitude should overflow into witness.
* Trust in God removes fear.

---

# Chapter 13 – Judgment Against Babylon

## Historical Background

At the time Isaiah prophesied, **Assyria** was the dominant empire. **Babylon** had not yet risen to world power. Remarkably, Isaiah foretells Babylon's downfall long before it became the empire that would later conquer Judah.

## Main Events

* God's judgment on Babylon is announced.
* The "Day of the LORD" is introduced.
* Nations are summoned as instruments of God's judgment.
* Babylon's future destruction is described.

## Important Teachings

Babylon symbolizes:

* Human pride
* Worldly power
* Rebellion against God

The historical fall of Babylon foreshadows God's final judgment on all evil (see Revelation 17–18).

## Spiritual Lessons

* No empire is beyond God's authority.
* Human glory is temporary.
* God's justice reaches every nation.

---

# Chapter 14 – The Fall of Babylon and the King of Babylon

## Main Events

* Israel's future restoration is promised.
* A taunt against the king of Babylon is given.
* The famous passage about the "morning star" appears.
* Assyria is also judged.
* Philistia receives a warning.

## The "Lucifer" Passage (Isaiah 14:12–15)

> "How you have fallen from heaven, O morning star, son of the dawn!"

# Overview of This Section – "The Book of Comfort"

Isaiah 40–55 marks a dramatic shift in the book. After chapters 1–39 focused largely on judgment, chapters 40–55 proclaim comfort, hope, redemption, and God's sovereign plan to restore His people.

Although Isaiah ministered during the eighth century BC, these chapters speak prophetically to the future generation of Jews who would be exiled in Babylon (586 BC). God comforts them in advance, assuring them that their captivity will end and that He will restore them through His chosen servant, Cyrus. Beyond the immediate return from exile, these chapters look forward to the coming of the Messiah, Jesus Christ, who would accomplish the greater redemption from sin.

This section is often called **"The Book of Comfort"** because it begins with the words:

> "Comfort, yes, comfort My people!" (Isaiah 40:1)

---

# Historical Background

Understanding this section is easier when viewed in chronological order.

| Event                                      | Approximate Date |
| ------------------------------------------ | ---------------- |
| Isaiah's ministry begins                   | c. 740 BC        |
| Assyria destroys Israel (Northern Kingdom) | 722 BC           |
| Isaiah prophesies concerning Judah         | 740–681 BC       |
| Babylon destroys Jerusalem                 | 586 BC           |
| Jews remain in Babylon for about 70 years  | 586–538 BC       |
| Cyrus conquers Babylon                     | 539 BC           |
| Cyrus allows the Jews to return            | 538 BC (Ezra 1)  |

Remarkably, Isaiah mentions **Cyrus by name nearly 150–200 years before he was born** (Isaiah 44:28; 45:1), demonstrating God's sovereign control over history.

# Summary of Chapters 40–55

These chapters reveal God's compassionate heart toward His covenant people and His sovereign plan to redeem both Israel and the nations. They move from comfort after exile to the promise of a Servant who would accomplish a far greater deliverance than release from Babylon—the redemption of humanity from sin. The climax is Isaiah 53, where the Suffering Servant bears the sins of many, followed by the universal invitation to salvation in Isaiah 55.

# Overview of This Section – The Future Glory of God's Kingdom

Isaiah 56–66 forms the final major division of the book. The themes move from **redemption to restoration**, **salvation to sanctification**, and **earthly Jerusalem to the eternal Kingdom of God**.

# Why Isaiah Is Called the "Fifth Gospel"

Many Bible scholars affectionately refer to Isaiah as the **"Fifth Gospel"** because no other Old Testament book presents such a comprehensive portrait of the Messiah.

Isaiah foretells:

* His virgin birth (7:14)
* His divine identity (9:6–7)
* His Spirit-empowered ministry (11:2; 61:1)
* His forerunner, John the Baptist (40:3)
* His compassion and justice (42:1–4)
* His rejection (53:3)
* His substitutionary suffering and death (53:4–6)
* His silence before His accusers (53:7)
* His burial with the rich (53:9)
* His resurrection and triumph (53:10–12)
* His mission to the Gentiles (49:6)
* His everlasting kingdom (9:7)

Because of these remarkable prophecies, Isaiah serves as a bridge between the Old and New Testaments, revealing God's redemptive plan centuries before the birth of Jesus Christ.

---

# Transition to Jeremiah

Isaiah's ministry ends before the fall of Jerusalem. He warned Judah to repent and foretold both the Babylonian exile and the future restoration. **Jeremiah** ministers about a century later during Judah's final years, witnessing the fulfillment of Isaiah's warnings as Jerusalem falls to Babylon in 586 BC. Together, Isaiah and Jeremiah demonstrate both the certainty of God's judgment and the faithfulness of His covenant promises.
