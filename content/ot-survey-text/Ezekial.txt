# Ezekiel Survey (Part 1)

---

# Introduction

The **Book of Ezekiel** is one of the four Major Prophets of the Old Testament (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Lamentations, and Ezekiel). It is the **26th book of the Bible** and contains **48 chapters**. The book records God's messages to His people during one of the darkest periods in Israel's history—the **Babylonian Exile**.

Unlike Jeremiah, who remained in Jerusalem until its destruction, **Ezekiel ministered among the Jewish exiles in Babylon**. While Jeremiah warned those still living in Judah, Ezekiel encouraged and rebuked those who had already been taken captive.

Ezekiel is a unique combination of:

* Prophecy
* Visions
* Symbolic actions
* Historical narratives
* Future restoration
* Messianic hope

More than any other prophet, Ezekiel emphasizes **the glory of God**. God's glory appears in the opening chapter, departs from the Temple because of Judah's sin (chapters 8–11), and returns to the future Temple in chapter 43. This movement forms the theological heart of the book.

The book can be divided into three major themes:

1. **Judgment on Judah (Chapters 1–24)**
2. **Judgment on the Nations (Chapters 25–32)**
3. **Hope and Restoration (Chapters 33–48)**

---

## Why Ezekiel Is Important

Ezekiel explains:

* Why Judah went into exile.
* How God remained sovereign even outside the Promised Land.
* Why God's glory departed from the Temple.
* God's promise to restore Israel.
* God's promise of a New Heart and New Spirit.
* The future Messianic Kingdom.
* The restoration of true worship.

Without Ezekiel, many New Testament teachings about spiritual rebirth, the Good Shepherd, resurrection, and the Kingdom of God would be more difficult to understand.

---

# Historical Background – Connecting the Previous Books

Understanding Ezekiel requires seeing where it fits in Israel's history.

## The Story So Far

### United Kingdom

* Saul became Israel's first king.
* David established Jerusalem as Israel's capital.
* Solomon built the First Temple.

---

### Divided Kingdom

After Solomon's death:

* Northern Kingdom = Israel
* Southern Kingdom = Judah

Both kingdoms repeatedly turned away from God.

---

### Fall of Israel

In **722 BC**, the Assyrians conquered the Northern Kingdom.

Only Judah remained.

---

### Jeremiah's Ministry

God sent Jeremiah to warn Judah.

For approximately **40 years**, Jeremiah pleaded with the nation to repent.

The people refused.

---

### Babylon Becomes the World Power

Babylon defeated Assyria and Egypt.

King **Nebuchadnezzar II** became ruler of the Babylonian Empire.

Judah became Babylon's servant.

---

Understanding these deportations is essential for reading Ezekiel.

| Year       | Event              | Important People                                              |

## Where Does Ezekiel Fit?

This timeline helps connect several Old Testament books.

| Book         | Location  | Time                                               |
| ------------ | --------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Jeremiah     | Jerusalem | Before and during Jerusalem's fall                 |
| Lamentations | Jerusalem | Immediately after the destruction                  |
| Ezekiel      | Babylon   | Among the exiles before and after Jerusalem's fall |

| Nehemiah     | Jerusalem | Rebuilding Jerusalem's walls                       |

Thus:

* Jeremiah warned **before** judgment.
* Lamentations mourned **after** judgment.
* Ezekiel explained **why** judgment occurred and proclaimed **future hope**.
* Daniel demonstrated faithful living in exile.
* Ezra and Nehemiah recorded restoration.

# Name of the Book

---

## Root Word

The Hebrew name combines two elements:

* **ḥazaq (חזק)** – "to strengthen" or "to make strong"
* **El (אֵל)** – God

---

## Literal Meaning

**"God Strengthens"**

or

**"Strengthened by God."**

---

## Why Is It Called Ezekiel?

The book bears the prophet's own name because it records:

* His prophetic ministry
* His visions
* His symbolic actions
* His messages from God
* His hope for Israel's restoration

The name beautifully reflects the prophet's calling.

Although Israel had become weak through sin, **God would strengthen His servant to strengthen His people.**

---

# Author of the Book

## Traditional Author

The traditional author is **the prophet Ezekiel**, son of Buzi.

He was both:

* A priest
* A prophet

This unique combination explains why the book gives extraordinary attention to:

* The Temple
* Worship
* Holiness
* Priestly duties
* God's glory

---

## Internal Evidence

The book repeatedly identifies Ezekiel as its author.

Examples include:

* Ezekiel 1:3
* Ezekiel 24:24
* Ezekiel 33:33

The frequent use of first-person narration strongly supports Ezekiel's authorship.

---

## External Evidence

Jewish tradition consistently accepted Ezekiel as the author.

Early Christian writers also affirmed this view.

The unity of language, style, historical setting, and theological emphasis strongly supports single authorship.

---

## Scholarly Discussion

Some modern scholars suggest later editorial additions.

However, the overwhelming literary unity of the book supports the traditional understanding that Ezekiel himself wrote the book, possibly with minor editorial organization after his ministry.

# Historical and Geographical Setting

The Book of Ezekiel is set during the **Babylonian Exile**, one of the most significant turning points in Israel's history. Unlike Isaiah, who prophesied before the exile, and Jeremiah, who ministered during Jerusalem's final days, Ezekiel served **among the Jewish captives in Babylon** after the second deportation (597 BC).

The people of Judah believed that because they possessed the Temple and the city of Jerusalem, God would never allow them to be conquered. However, generations of idolatry, injustice, covenant-breaking, and rebellion against God's prophets brought divine judgment. Through Ezekiel, God explained that the exile was not a sign of His weakness but an act of His righteous discipline.

Ezekiel received his first vision while living near the **Chebar (Kebar) Canal** in Babylon (Ezekiel 1:1–3). This was shocking to the exiles because it demonstrated that **God's glory was not confined to Jerusalem**. The Lord could reveal Himself anywhere, even in a foreign land.

Throughout the book, Ezekiel addresses three periods:

1. **Before Jerusalem's destruction (593–586 BC)** – Warnings that the city would fall.
2. **The fall of Jerusalem (586 BC)** – Confirmation that God's judgment had come.
3. **After Jerusalem's fall** – Messages of hope, restoration, and the future Messianic Kingdom.

---

## Political Background

Several world powers shaped Ezekiel's ministry.

### Assyrian Empire (Before Ezekiel)

* Destroyed the Northern Kingdom (Israel) in **722 BC**.
* Demonstrated God's judgment upon covenant unfaithfulness.

### Babylonian Empire

Under **King Nebuchadnezzar II**, Babylon became the dominant empire of the ancient Near East.

Babylon invaded Judah three times:

| Year       | Event                                 |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------- |
| **605 BC** | First deportation (Daniel taken)      |
| **597 BC** | Second deportation (Ezekiel taken)    |
| **586 BC** | Jerusalem destroyed and Temple burned |

During Ezekiel's ministry, Judah no longer existed as an independent kingdom.

---

## Cultural Setting

The Jewish exiles faced enormous challenges.

They had lost:

* Their homeland
* Their Temple
* Their king
* Their national identity
* Their sense of security

Many wondered:

* Has God abandoned us?
* Can we worship outside Jerusalem?
* Are His promises finished?
* Will Israel ever exist again?

Ezekiel answered these questions by revealing that:

* God remained sovereign.
* His covenant promises still stood.
* Restoration would come in God's timing.

---

## Religious Condition of the People

Spiritually, Judah had fallen into serious sin.

Common sins included:

* Idol worship
* Child sacrifice
* Corrupt priests
* False prophets
* Social injustice
* Sabbath-breaking
* Religious hypocrisy
* Pride
* Trust in political alliances instead of God

One of Ezekiel's central messages is:

> **The problem was never God's unfaithfulness—it was Israel's rebellion.**

Yet God also promised:

> "I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit within you" (Ezekiel 36:26).

The Book of Ezekiel was written to explain God's judgment, reveal His holiness, and proclaim His promise of restoration.

## Why Was the Book Written?

God raised up Ezekiel to:

* Explain why Judah went into exile.
* Warn that Jerusalem would fall.
* Expose the nation's sin.
* Condemn false prophets.
* Declare judgment on surrounding nations.
* Encourage faithful exiles.
* Reveal God's future restoration of Israel.

# Ezekiel Survey (Part 3)

# Chapter-by-Chapter Summary (Chapters 1–12)

The first twelve chapters introduce Ezekiel's prophetic ministry and contain warnings given **before the destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC**. God reveals His glory, commissions Ezekiel as a watchman, and uses dramatic symbolic acts to demonstrate that Judah's judgment is certain because of persistent rebellion.

---

# Chapter 1 – The Vision of God's Glory

### Main Events

* Ezekiel receives his first vision by the **Chebar Canal** in Babylon (1:1–3).
* A great storm approaches from the north.
* Four living creatures (cherubim) appear, each with four faces (man, lion, ox, eagle) and four wings.
* Wheels within wheels accompany the creatures, symbolizing God's omnipresent activity.
* Above them is a crystal-like expanse and a sapphire throne.
* Ezekiel sees the radiant appearance of **the glory of the LORD** and falls on his face.

### Important Teachings

* God's glory is not limited to Jerusalem.
* God reigns over all creation and nations.
* His sovereignty extends even into exile.

### Key Persons

* Ezekiel
* The four living creatures (cherubim)
* God

### Spiritual Lessons

* God is present wherever His people are.
* Worship begins with reverence for God's holiness.
* Even in difficult circumstances, God reveals Himself.

---

# Chapter 2 – Ezekiel's Commission

### Main Events

* God addresses Ezekiel as **"Son of Man,"** a title used over ninety times in the book.
* Ezekiel is sent to a rebellious nation.
* He receives a scroll filled with words of lamentation and judgment.
* God commands him not to fear opposition.

### Important Teachings

* A prophet must faithfully proclaim God's Word.
* Success is measured by obedience, not by people's response.

### Key Persons

* Ezekiel
* God

### Spiritual Lessons

* God's servants must speak truth courageously.
* Obedience requires faithfulness even when rejected.

---

# Chapter 3 – The Watchman for Israel

### Main Events

* Ezekiel eats the scroll, symbolizing complete acceptance of God's message.
* The Spirit empowers him.
* God appoints him as Israel's **watchman**.
* Ezekiel is held accountable to warn sinners.
* He remains silent for seven days, overwhelmed by his calling.

### Important Teachings

* Spiritual leaders bear responsibility for warning others.
* God's Word must first be internalized before it is proclaimed.

### Key Persons

* Ezekiel
* God

### Spiritual Lessons

* Leaders must know God's Word before teaching it.
* Silence in the face of sin can bring accountability.

---

# Chapter 4 – The Siege of Jerusalem Illustrated

### Main Events

God commands Ezekiel to perform symbolic actions:

* Draw Jerusalem on a clay tablet.
* Build miniature siege works.
* Lie on his left side for **390 days**, representing Israel's sin.
* Lie on his right side for **40 days**, representing Judah's sin.
* Eat measured food and drink limited water.
* Bake bread under difficult conditions to symbolize defilement.

### Important Teachings

* Judgment is certain.
* Sin has long-term consequences.
* God's warnings are illustrated through vivid signs.

### Key Persons

* Ezekiel

### Spiritual Lessons

* God patiently warns before judging.
* Symbolic actions can communicate profound spiritual truths.

---

# Chapter 5 – Judgment Against Jerusalem

### Main Events

* Ezekiel shaves his head and beard.
* The hair is divided into three parts:

  * Burned
  * Struck with the sword
  * Scattered to the wind
* A few hairs are preserved, symbolizing the faithful remnant.

### Important Teachings

* Jerusalem's punishment is deserved.
* God always preserves a faithful remnant.

### Key Persons

* Ezekiel
* The people of Jerusalem

### Spiritual Lessons

* God disciplines His people because of covenant unfaithfulness.
* Mercy is always present, even in judgment.

---

# Chapter 6 – Judgment on the Mountains of Israel

* Repentance begins when people recognize their sin.

### Key Persons

* Ezekiel
* The Israelites

## Christ in Ezekiel 1–12

Even in these chapters of judgment, we see foreshadows of Christ:

* **The "Son of Man"** title anticipates Jesus, who frequently used this title for Himself (cf. Matthew 8:20).
* **The faithful remnant** points to those preserved by God's grace through faith.

# Ezekiel Survey (Part 6 – Final)

# Chapter-by-Chapter Summary (Chapters 40–48)

The final nine chapters of Ezekiel form one continuous vision given in the **25th year of the exile (573 BC)** (Ezekiel 40:1). These chapters shift entirely from judgment to hope. Ezekiel is shown a magnificent future temple, renewed worship, a restored land, and a city where God's presence permanently dwells.

These chapters have been interpreted in different ways by faithful Christian scholars:

* **Literal (Premillennial) View:** A future millennial temple will be built during Christ's earthly reign.
* **Symbolic / Typological View:** The temple symbolizes God's restored presence among His people, ultimately fulfilled in Christ, the Church, and finally the New Jerusalem.
* **Combination View:** Some elements may be literal while others are symbolic.

Regardless of interpretation, the central message remains the same:

> **God will once again dwell with His redeemed people forever.**

# Summary of the Book

Ezekiel presents one of the Bible's clearest portraits of both God's holiness and His mercy. Through dramatic visions, symbolic actions, and prophetic messages, Ezekiel explains why Judah went into exile: persistent covenant unfaithfulness. Yet judgment is not the end of the story. God promises to gather His people, cleanse them, give them a new heart and Spirit, raise them from spiritual death, and dwell among them forever.

The book moves from **judgment (chapters 1–24)**, to **judgment on the nations (25–32)**, to **hope and restoration (33–48)**, culminating in the promise that **"The LORD Is There"** (48:35).

# Additional Important Information

## Literary Structure

* Vision reports
* Symbolic actions
* Judgment oracles
* Restoration prophecies
* Apocalyptic imagery

## Important Symbols

* Four living creatures
* Wheels within wheels
* Watchman
* Dry bones
* Shepherd
* New heart
* River of Life
* Temple
* Glory of God

## Covenantal Emphasis

Ezekiel emphasizes that God's covenant faithfulness endures despite Israel's failure. The promise of a new heart anticipates the New Covenant.

## Hebrew Insights

* **Kābôd (כָּבוֹד)** – "Glory," referring to God's majestic presence.
* **Rûaḥ (רוּחַ)** – "Spirit," "breath," or "wind," a key term especially in chapter 37.
* **Yahweh Shammah (יְהוָה שָׁמָּה)** – "The LORD Is There" (48:35).

## Connections to the Gospel

* Jesus is the Good Shepherd.
* Jesus fulfills the promise of the New Covenant.
* The Holy Spirit gives the new heart promised in Ezekiel.
* Christ is the true Temple in whom God dwells with His people, and Revelation echoes Ezekiel's vision of the restored creation.

## Archaeological Insights

* Babylonian records confirm the exile of King Jehoiachin in 597 BC.
* Excavations illuminate the Babylonian world in which Ezekiel ministered, supporting the historical setting of the book.

## Study Notes for Bible Teachers

* Emphasize the transition from judgment to hope.
* Explain symbolic visions in their historical context before exploring prophetic applications.
* Present major interpretive views of the temple vision respectfully, keeping the focus on the certainty of God's presence and ultimate restoration.

# Conclusion

The Book of Ezekiel is a profound testimony to God's holiness, justice, and unfailing mercy. Though Judah experienced exile because of persistent rebellion, God never abandoned His covenant purposes. Through visions of restoration, a new heart, a faithful Shepherd, and a renewed dwelling place, Ezekiel points readers toward the ultimate hope fulfilled in Jesus Christ and consummated in God's eternal kingdom.

For Bible teachers, pastors, students, and believers, Ezekiel offers enduring lessons on repentance, faithful leadership, the work of the Holy Spirit, and the assurance that God's presence is the greatest blessing His people can receive. The book closes not with despair, but with one of Scripture's most hope-filled declarations:

> **"The name of the city from that day shall be, 'The LORD Is There.'" (Ezekiel 48:35)**

This final promise reminds every generation that God's ultimate purpose is to dwell forever with His redeemed people.
