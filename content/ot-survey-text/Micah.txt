# Micah Survey

---

# Introduction

The **Book of Micah** is the sixth book in the collection of the **Twelve Minor Prophets** in the Old Testament. It contains seven chapters and combines messages of **judgment, justice, repentance, hope, restoration, and messianic expectation**.

Micah ministered primarily during the **8th century BC**, during the reigns of **Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah**, kings of Judah (Micah 1:1). His ministry overlapped with that of **Isaiah and possibly Hosea**.

Micah addressed both **Samaria**, the capital of the northern kingdom of Israel, and **Jerusalem**, the capital of Judah. His message exposed the sins of rulers, priests, prophets, wealthy landowners, and ordinary people who exploited others.

Yet Micah's message was not only about judgment. He also proclaimed God's future restoration and contains several important passages concerning the **coming ruler from Bethlehem**, the restoration of God's people, and God's covenant faithfulness.

One of the best-known statements in the book is:

> **"He has told you, O man, what is good; and what does the LORD require of you but to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?"**
> — Micah 6:8

Micah matters to Christians today because it teaches that genuine faith cannot be separated from **justice, mercy, humility, holiness, and faithful obedience to God**.

---

# Name of the Book

The Hebrew name is:

**מִיכָה — Mîkhāh / Mikhah**

It is a shortened form of a Hebrew name associated with:

**מִיכָיָהוּ — Mîkhāyāhû**

### Meaning

The name **Micah** means:

> **"Who is like the LORD?"**

It is formed from:

* **מי (mi)** — "who?"
* **כ (kə/kā)** — "like/as"
* **יה (Yah)** — a shortened form of the divine name YHWH

The meaning is closely connected with the question found at the end of the book:

> **"Who is a God like you, pardoning iniquity..."**
> — **Micah 7:18**

This creates a beautiful literary connection between **Micah's name** and the book's conclusion.

### Why is the book called Micah?

The book is named after its principal prophet:

> **"The word of the LORD that came to Micah of Moresheth..."**
> — Micah 1:1

---

# Author of the Book

## Traditional Author

The traditional author is:

**Micah of Moresheth**

He was a prophet from **Moresheth**, a town in the Judean lowlands.

> **Micah 1:1**

Micah's name and hometown distinguish him from other biblical figures named Micah.

## Internal Evidence

Micah 1:1 identifies:

* The prophet — Micah
* His hometown — Moresheth
* His ministry — during the reigns of Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah
* His prophetic audience — Samaria and Jerusalem

Micah speaks in the first person in several passages:

> **Micah 3:8**

He identifies himself as one empowered by the Spirit of the LORD to proclaim God's message.

## External Historical Evidence

Micah is explicitly referred to in the Old Testament in **Jeremiah 26:18–19**.

Jeremiah records that elders cited Micah's prophecy concerning Jerusalem:

> "Zion shall be plowed as a field..."

They remembered that **Hezekiah responded by fearing the LORD** rather than executing Micah.

This is significant because it provides an early biblical witness to Micah's prophetic ministry.

## Scholarly Opinions

Most scholars recognize the book as rooted in the ministry of the historical prophet Micah.

There is discussion about whether every portion of the present book originated directly from Micah himself or whether later prophetic editors shaped and preserved some material.

Particular discussion often concerns **Micah 7:7–20** and sections concerning restoration.

The major scholarly positions include:

1. **Essentially unified prophetic work** rooted in Micah's ministry.
2. **A prophetic collection** containing Micah's original preaching together with later editorial arrangement.
3. **Some later additions or expansions** reflecting post-exilic hopes.

Regardless of these debates, the canonical book presents a unified theological message concerning **judgment, justice, repentance, mercy, restoration, and God's covenant faithfulness**.

# Date Written

## Approximate Date

Micah's ministry occurred approximately:

**8th century BC, c. 740–700 BC**

Micah 1:1 specifically places his ministry during the reigns of:

* **Jotham**
* **Ahaz**
* **Hezekiah**

of Judah.

| King     | Approximate Reign | Relevance to Micah                     |

| Ahaz     | c. 735–715 BC     | Political and spiritual crisis         |
| Hezekiah | c. 715–686 BC     | Assyrian pressure and religious reform |

Exact dates vary somewhat depending on chronological reconstruction.

## Important Historical Background

Micah lived during a period of enormous political instability.

The major international power was:

**Assyria**

Assyria expanded aggressively throughout the ancient Near East.

Major events included:

* Assyrian expansion into the Levant
* The Syro-Ephraimite crisis
* Assyrian attacks against Israel and Judah
* The fall of **Samaria in 722 BC**
* Assyrian invasion of Judah during Hezekiah's reign

Micah therefore preached during a time when God's people faced both **internal injustice and external military danger**.

# Historical and Geographical Setting

## Historical Context

Micah ministered in **Judah**, particularly in the region surrounding Jerusalem, while addressing both kingdoms.

His hometown, **Moresheth**, was located in the **Shephelah**, the lowland region between Judah's hill country and the coastal plain.

This geographical location placed Micah close to important military and economic routes.

## Political Background

Judah and Israel lived under increasing Assyrian pressure.

The northern kingdom eventually fell:

> **2 Kings 17:5–6**

Samaria fell to Assyria around **722 BC**.

Judah itself was later attacked by Assyria under **Sennacherib** during Hezekiah's reign:

> **2 Kings 18–19**

Micah's prophecies concerning Jerusalem therefore had significant historical relevance.

## Social Conditions

Micah strongly condemns social exploitation.

The wealthy seized:

* Houses
* Fields
* Inheritance

> **Micah 2:1–2**

The leaders were supposed to protect justice but instead exploited the people.

> **Micah 3:1–3**

Religious leaders were also corrupt.

> **Micah 3:5–7, 11**

Micah therefore presents a society in which **economic injustice, political corruption, and religious hypocrisy** had become intertwined.

## Religious Condition

The people continued religious activity but often combined it with injustice.

Micah 6:6–7 describes increasingly extravagant religious offerings:

* Burnt offerings
* Calves
* Thousands of rams
* Rivers of oil
* Even one's firstborn

But God identifies what He truly requires:

> **Justice, kindness, and humble walking with God.**

# Key Verses

## Micah 1:2

**Verse Text:**

> "Hear, you peoples, all of you; pay attention, O earth, and all that is in it..."

**Explanation:**
Micah calls the entire world to listen to God's covenant lawsuit.

**Significance:**
Israel's actions have universal theological significance because the LORD is sovereign over all creation.

---

## Micah 2:2

**Verse Text:**

> "They covet fields and seize them, and houses, and take them away..."

**Explanation:**
Micah condemns powerful people who exploit others economically.

**Significance:**
God cares about property, economic justice, and the treatment of vulnerable people.

---

## Micah 3:8

**Verse Text:**

> "But as for me, I am filled with power, with the Spirit of the LORD..."

**Explanation:**
Micah contrasts genuine prophetic ministry with corrupt prophets.

**Significance:**
Biblical prophetic ministry is grounded in God's truth rather than personal gain.

---

## Micah 4:2

**Verse Text:**

> "For out of Zion shall go forth the law, and the word of the LORD from Jerusalem."

**Explanation:**
Micah describes a future time when nations will come to learn God's ways.

**Significance:**
The verse contributes to the biblical vision of God's kingdom and worldwide recognition of His rule.

---

## Micah 5:2

**Verse Text:**

> "But you, O Bethlehem Ephrathah, who are too little to be among the clans of Judah, from you shall come forth for me one who is to be ruler in Israel..."

**Explanation:**
A future ruler is associated with Bethlehem.

**Significance:**
Matthew identifies this passage with the birth of Jesus:

> **Matthew 2:5–6**

---

## Micah 6:8

**Verse Text:**

> "He has told you, O man, what is good; and what does the LORD require of you but to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?"

**Explanation:**
God's covenant requirements cannot be replaced by outward religious performance.

**Significance:**
This is one of the Bible's clearest summaries of practical covenant faithfulness.

---

## Micah 7:18

**Verse Text:**

> "Who is a God like you, pardoning iniquity and passing over transgression..."

**Explanation:**
The book concludes by celebrating God's incomparable mercy.

**Significance:**
Micah's name and the book's conclusion form a beautiful theological connection.

# Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

## Chapter 1 – Judgment on Samaria and Jerusalem

### Main Events

Micah receives the word of the LORD during the reigns of Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah.

God summons the nations as witnesses.

> **Micah 1:2**

The LORD comes against Samaria because of Israel's sin.

Samaria's judgment is followed by warnings concerning Judah and Jerusalem.

Micah mourns over the coming disaster and names numerous towns.

### Important Teachings

* God sees national sin.
* Religious privilege does not eliminate accountability.
* Political power cannot protect people from God's judgment.
* God's judgment can begin among His covenant people.

### Spiritual Lessons

1. God takes sin seriously.
2. Leaders are accountable to God.
3. God's people cannot rely on religious identity while practicing injustice.

---

# Chapter 2 – Judgment on Oppressors

Micah condemns people who plan evil against others.

> **Micah 2:1**

They covet fields and houses and seize them.

> **Micah 2:2**

This is a powerful condemnation of **economic exploitation**.

The chapter also condemns prophets who tell people what they want to hear.

> **Micah 2:11**

Yet the chapter contains a promise of future gathering and restoration:

> **Micah 2:12–13**

### Spiritual Lessons

* Greed is spiritually destructive.
* God cares about economic justice.
* Religious leaders must proclaim God's truth rather than profitable messages.
* Judgment does not eliminate God's promise of restoration.

---

# Chapter 3 – Judgment on Israel's Leaders

Micah directly addresses:

* Rulers
* Prophets
* Priests

He accuses leaders of abusing the people they were supposed to protect.

> **Micah 3:1–3**

False prophets are condemned because their message changes according to financial reward.

> **Micah 3:5**

The leaders are described as ruling:

> "for a bribe"

> **Micah 3:11**

Yet they still claim:

> "Is not the LORD in the midst of us?"

Micah warns:

> **"Therefore because of you Zion shall be plowed as a field..."**
> — Micah 3:12

This verse is later quoted in **Jeremiah 26:18**.

### Spiritual Lessons

* Leadership is stewardship.
* Spiritual office does not excuse corruption.
* God's presence should never be used as a justification for sin.
* Teachers and pastors have a responsibility to speak truthfully.

---

# Chapter 4 – Future Restoration and Peace

The tone dramatically changes.

Micah describes a future time when:

> "the mountain of the house of the LORD shall be established"

> **Micah 4:1**

Nations will come to learn God's ways.

> **Micah 4:2**

The famous vision follows:

> **"They shall beat their swords into plowshares..."**
> — Micah 4:3

The passage describes:

* International peace
* God's instruction
* Justice
* Security
* Restoration
* God's reign

Micah also describes God's people being gathered and restored.

### Important Teaching

God's final purposes are not exhausted by present judgment.

The biblical pattern is:

> **Judgment → purification → restoration**

---

# Chapter 5 – The Coming Ruler from Bethlehem

Micah 5 contains one of the book's most important messianic passages.

> **Micah 5:2**

Bethlehem Ephrathah is described as small among Judah's clans, yet from it will come a ruler.

The ruler's origins are described as being:

> "from of old, from ancient days"

The ruler will shepherd God's people.

> **Micah 5:4**

He will bring security and greatness.

### New Testament Connection

Matthew's Gospel explicitly connects this prophecy with Jesus' birthplace:

> **Matthew 2:5–6**

The passage is therefore one of the significant Old Testament texts used in the New Testament's presentation of Jesus as the promised ruler.

### Spiritual Lessons

* God often works through what appears insignificant.
* God's kingdom does not depend on worldly greatness.
* The Messiah is presented as both ruler and shepherd.

---

# Chapter 6 – God's Covenant Lawsuit

Micah presents a courtroom-like scene.

God calls:

* Mountains
* Hills
* The foundations of the earth

as witnesses.

> **Micah 6:1–2**

God reminds Israel of His faithfulness:

* Bringing them from Egypt
* Redeeming them from slavery
* Providing leadership

> **Micah 6:3–5**

The people ask what they should bring to God.

They propose increasingly extravagant offerings.

> **Micah 6:6–7**

God's response is:

> **Micah 6:8**

### Micah 6:8

The LORD requires:

1. **Do justice**
2. **Love kindness**
3. **Walk humbly with your God**

This does not mean sacrifice was inherently wrong. Rather, religious ritual without covenant obedience is unacceptable.

### Judgment for Injustice

Micah then returns to:

* Dishonest scales
* Violence
* Lies
* Wealth obtained through injustice

> **Micah 6:10–12**

### Spiritual Lessons

* Worship must be accompanied by righteousness.
* God cares about honesty in business.
* Humility is central to faithful discipleship.
* Justice and mercy belong together.

---

# Chapter 7 – Hope, Faith, and God's Mercy

Micah begins chapter 7 by describing the moral collapse of society.

> **Micah 7:1–6**

But Micah personally responds:

> **"But as for me, I will look to the LORD; I will wait for the God of my salvation..."**
> — Micah 7:7

This is a major statement of faith.

Micah expresses hope despite darkness:

> **Micah 7:8**

The chapter ends with confidence in God's forgiveness.

> **Micah 7:18–19**

God is described as One who:

* Pardons iniquity
* Forgives transgression
* Delights in steadfast love
* Has compassion
* Casts sins into the depths of the sea

### Final Message

The book that began with judgment ends with **mercy and covenant faithfulness**.

## 1. God Cares About Justice

Micah shows that justice is not merely a political or social concept.

It is a biblical concern.

> **Micah 6:8**

## 2. God Rejects Hypocritical Worship

Religious activity cannot compensate for:

* Oppression
* Dishonesty
* Violence
* Greed
* Pride

> **Micah 6:6–8**

---

## 3. Leaders Have Greater Responsibility

Micah's strongest criticisms are directed toward leaders.

> **Micah 3:1–12**

Christian leaders should therefore pursue:

* Integrity
* Truth
* Servanthood
* Accountability
* Justice

---

## 4. God Defends the Vulnerable

Micah condemns the seizure of people's homes and land.

> **Micah 2:1–2**

God does not merely require outward religious performance.

He requires people to:

> "walk humbly with your God."

> **Micah 6:8**

---

The book points toward:

* Restoration

* God's mercy

## 7. Wait for God

Micah declares:

> **"I will look to the LORD; I will wait for the God of my salvation."**

> **Micah 7:7**

## 8. God's Mercy Is Greater Than Our Failure

The closing verses emphasize God's forgiveness.

> **Micah 7:18–19**

# Foreshadows / Types of Jesus Christ

Micah contains significant messianic material.

## 1. The Ruler from Bethlehem

> **Micah 5:2**

The prophecy identifies **Bethlehem Ephrathah** as the place from which a future ruler will come.

Matthew explicitly connects this passage with Jesus:

> **Matthew 2:5–6**

This is one of the clearest connections between Micah and the birth narratives of Jesus.

---

## 2. The Shepherd-King

Micah says:

> "He shall stand and shepherd his flock in the strength of the LORD."

> **Micah 5:4**

This provides a strong biblical background for Jesus' description of Himself as:

> **"the good shepherd"**

> **John 10:11**

The New Testament also identifies Jesus as:

* The **Good Shepherd** — John 10:11
* The **Great Shepherd** — Hebrews 13:20
* The **Chief Shepherd** — 1 Peter 5:4

---

## 3. Ruler of God's People

Micah's coming ruler will exercise authority over God's people.

This contributes to the Old Testament expectation of a **Davidic ruler** and finds its Christian fulfillment in the New Testament presentation of Jesus as Messiah and King.

---

## 4. God's Universal Kingdom

Micah 4:1–5 describes nations coming to the LORD's instruction.

This theme connects with the New Testament vision of Christ's kingdom extending among all nations:

* **Matthew 28:19**
* **Acts 1:8**
* **Revelation 7:9–10**

### Important Note

Prophecy in Micah sometimes has **near-term historical judgment** alongside **future restoration and messianic expectation**.

This means individual passages should be interpreted within the larger prophetic pattern rather than assuming that every verse refers exclusively to one future event.

---

# Summary of the Book

The Book of Micah presents a powerful combination of **judgment and hope**.

Micah condemns:

* Corrupt leaders
* Economic exploitation
* Religious hypocrisy
* Dishonesty
* Violence
* Covenant unfaithfulness

But he also announces:

* Restoration
* Peace
* God's kingdom
* The preservation of a remnant
* A future ruler from Bethlehem
* God's forgiveness

The book's central ethical summary is:

> **"Do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God."**
> — Micah 6:8

Its theological climax is:

> **"Who is a God like you, pardoning iniquity..."**
> — Micah 7:18

Together these passages demonstrate that **biblical justice and biblical mercy belong together**.

### Main Theological Message

God is holy and will judge injustice, but He remains faithful to His covenant promises and delights in showing mercy to those who turn to Him.

### Spiritual Takeaway

Micah calls believers to:

> **Live justly, love mercy, walk humbly, trust God's promises, and hope in His redemption.**

# Additional Important Information

## 1. The Book Has a Covenant-Lawsuit Pattern

Micah contains language resembling a legal courtroom.

God summons creation as a witness:

> **Micah 6:1–2**

This is sometimes called a **covenant lawsuit** or *rîb* pattern.

The basic movement is:

**God's covenant faithfulness → Israel's failure → accusation → judgment → hope**

---

## 2. Micah 6:8 Should Not Be Separated from Its Context

Micah 6:8 is sometimes quoted independently as a general statement about morality.

While it certainly has broad application, its immediate context is much richer.

The surrounding passage contrasts:

**religious offerings**

with

**covenant obedience**.

The issue is not that worship or sacrifice is inherently meaningless.

Rather:

> **External religious practices cannot substitute for obedience to God.**

---

## 3. Justice and Mercy Are Not Opposites

Micah teaches both:

**God's justice**

and

**God's mercy.**

The book does not eliminate one in favor of the other.

Compare:

* **Micah 2–3** — judgment
* **Micah 4–5** — restoration
* **Micah 6** — covenant accountability
* **Micah 7** — forgiveness

---

## 4. Micah's Message to Leaders

Micah 3 is especially important for church leadership.

Leaders are accountable for how they use authority.

The biblical pattern is not:

> **Power → privilege**

but:

> **Authority → responsibility → service → accountability**

This principle is consistent with New Testament teaching about Christian leadership:

* **Mark 10:42–45**
* **1 Peter 5:2–3**

---

## 5. Micah and Isaiah

Micah and Isaiah ministered during overlapping historical periods.

Both contain strikingly similar kingdom language.

Compare:

> **Micah 4:1–3**

with

> **Isaiah 2:2–4**

Both describe nations coming to God's mountain and the transformation of weapons into instruments of agriculture.

This indicates a shared prophetic vision of God's future reign and peace.

---

## 6. Micah and Jeremiah

Jeremiah 26:18–19 specifically recalls Micah.

Micah had warned:

> "Zion shall be plowed as a field..."

The elders remembered this prophecy during Jeremiah's ministry and noted that **Hezekiah responded with fear of the LORD**.

This provides an important historical connection between the prophetic ministries.

---

## 7. Micah and the Birth of Jesus

Micah 5:2 is particularly important for Christian biblical theology.

Matthew 2:5–6 identifies **Bethlehem** as the location associated with the Messiah's birth.

The passage emphasizes that God's promised ruler comes from a place that appears insignificant.

This becomes part of the New Testament presentation of Jesus as the promised Messiah.

---

## 8. Micah's Message to the Church

Micah offers several important principles for churches:

### Worship

Worship must be accompanied by obedience.

### Leadership

Leaders must reject exploitation and corruption.

### Mission

God's purposes include the nations.

### Social Responsibility

Believers should care about injustice and vulnerable people.

### Personal Holiness

Humility before God is essential.

### Hope

Christians can trust God's promises even in times of darkness.

---

## 9. Important Hebrew Concepts

### Mîkhāh — מִיכָה

**"Who is like the LORD?"**

Connected conceptually with:

> **Micah 7:18**

---

### Hesed — חֶסֶד

Often translated:

* steadfast love
* lovingkindness
* covenant loyalty
* mercy

Micah's message repeatedly emphasizes God's covenant faithfulness.

---

### Mishpat — מִשְׁפָּט

Often translated:

> **justice / judgment**

It is central to the ethical demand of:

> **Micah 6:8**

---

### Tsedeqah — צְדָקָה

Associated with:

> **righteousness**

Justice and righteousness are closely related themes throughout the prophetic literature.

---

## 10. Important Symbols

### Mountain of the LORD

Represents the future prominence of God's rule and instruction.

> **Micah 4:1–2**

### Swords into Plowshares

Symbolizes the transformation from war to peace.

> **Micah 4:3**

### Shepherd

Represents God's care and the promised ruler's leadership.

> **Micah 5:4**

### Remnant

Represents the people God preserves through judgment.

> **Micah 4:7; 5:7–8**

### Sea / Depths

In Micah 7:19, the depths of the sea symbolize the complete removal of sin:

> **"You will cast all our sins into the depths of the sea."**

---

## 11. Three Major Questions for Bible Teachers

Micah can be organized around three major questions:

### Question 1: What happens when God's people reject justice?

**Micah 1–3**

Answer:

> Judgment and accountability.

### Question 2: What does God's future look like?

**Micah 4–5**

Answer:

> Restoration, peace, God's reign, and the coming ruler.

### Question 3: What does God desire from His people?

**Micah 6–7**

Answer:

> Justice, mercy, humility, faith, repentance, and trust in God's covenant faithfulness.

---

# Conclusion

The Book of Micah is a powerful prophetic message for anyone who wants to understand **God's holiness, justice, mercy, covenant faithfulness, and redemptive purposes**.

Micah refuses to separate worship from everyday life. A person cannot claim to honor God while exploiting others, practicing dishonesty, abusing authority, or ignoring injustice.

The heart of the book's ethical message is beautifully summarized:

> **"Do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God."**
> — **Micah 6:8**

Yet Micah does not leave God's people under judgment. The prophet points toward a future of **peace, restoration, God's kingdom, and a ruler from Bethlehem**.

For Christian readers, **Micah 5:2** becomes especially significant because Matthew connects Bethlehem with the birth of Jesus Christ.

And the book closes not with despair but with one of Scripture's great celebrations of God's mercy:

> **"Who is a God like you, pardoning iniquity..."**
> — **Micah 7:18**

Micah therefore moves from **sin to judgment, from judgment to hope, and from hope to mercy**.

Its enduring message is clear:

**God calls His people to live with justice, love with mercy, walk with humility, remain faithful in difficult times, and place their hope in the God who forgives and restores.**
