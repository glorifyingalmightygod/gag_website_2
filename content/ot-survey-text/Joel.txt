# Introduction

## Historical Connection to Previous Books

As we move from **Hosea** to **Joel**, it is important to understand that although Joel appears second among the Minor Prophets, **his ministry may have occurred before Hosea**. The Minor Prophets are **not arranged strictly in chronological order**.

After the reign of **King Solomon** (c. 931 BC), Israel divided into two kingdoms:

| Kingdom                   | Capital   | Tribes           | First King |
| ------------------------- | --------- | ---------------- | ---------- |
| Northern Kingdom (Israel) | Samaria   | 10 Tribes        | Jeroboam I |
| Southern Kingdom (Judah)  | Jerusalem | Judah & Benjamin | Rehoboam   |

During the next two centuries, God continually raised up prophets to call His people back to covenant faithfulness.

One possible sequence is:

* **Elijah** – Prophet to Israel (did not write a prophetic book)
* **Elisha** – Successor to Elijah (did not write a prophetic book)
* **Joel** – Prophet primarily to Judah (likely one of the earliest writing prophets)
* **Amos** – Prophet to Israel
* **Hosea** – Prophet to Israel
* **Isaiah** – Prophet to Judah
* **Micah** – Prophet to Judah and Israel

Unlike Hosea, Joel does not mention Assyria, Babylon, or any reigning king. This makes dating the book difficult, but it also gives the book a timeless quality.

---

## When Did Joel Live?

Joel is one of the most difficult books in the Bible to date because it contains very few historical references.

### Major Scholarly Views

| View                                    | Approximate Date | Reason                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Early Date (Most Conservative View)** | **835–800 BC**   | Temple worship is active, priests are prominent, no mention of Assyria or Babylon, and no reigning king is named. This fits the period when King Joash was a child under the guidance of the high priest Jehoiada. |
| **Late Date**                           | **500–400 BC**   | Some scholars argue for a post-exilic setting because Joel speaks of scattered Israelites and surrounding nations.                                                                                                 |

For this survey, we will follow the **early-date view**, which is held by many conservative evangelical scholars. However, readers should know that the exact date cannot be established with certainty.

## Historical Background

The kingdom of Judah had gone through a turbulent period.

After the godly reign of **King Jehoshaphat**, several wicked rulers led the nation away from God.

* **Jehoram** introduced idolatry.
* **Ahaziah** ruled briefly.
* **Athaliah**, the daughter of Ahab and Jezebel, attempted to destroy the royal family of David so she could seize the throne.
* God preserved His covenant by protecting the infant **Joash**, who was hidden in the Temple for six years.
* The high priest **Jehoiada** later crowned Joash king and restored true worship (2 Kings 11; 2 Chronicles 22–24).

It was during this period of national recovery that Judah experienced a devastating natural disaster.

---

## The Great Locust Plague

Joel begins by describing an unprecedented invasion of locusts.

These insects destroyed:

* Grain fields
* Vineyards
* Olive groves
* Fruit trees
* Pastures

The devastation affected every level of society:

* Farmers lost their crops.
* Shepherds lost grazing land.
* Families faced famine.
* Priests could no longer offer grain and drink offerings in the Temple because there was nothing left to offer.

Joel recognized that this was not merely an agricultural crisis. It was a divine warning calling the nation to repentance.

The locust plague became a picture of a far greater coming judgment known as **the Day of the LORD**.

---

## What Is the Book About?

The Book of Joel begins with a disaster that everyone could see—a plague of locusts.

But Joel looks beyond the immediate event.

He teaches that the destruction caused by the locusts points to a greater spiritual reality.

God was warning His people that if they continued in sin, a more severe judgment would come.

Yet Joel's message is not one of hopelessness.

He repeatedly calls the people to:

* Return to the Lord.
* Repent sincerely.
* Trust in God's mercy.

The second half of the book shifts from judgment to hope.

Joel promises that:

* God will restore what has been lost.
* God will pour out His Spirit on His people.
* God will judge the nations.
* God will establish His righteous kingdom.

Thus, Joel moves from **crisis to restoration**, showing that God's mercy is available to all who truly seek Him.

Joel is one of the foundational prophetic books because it introduces themes that echo throughout the rest of Scripture.

These include:

* The **Day of the LORD**, a major prophetic theme developed by later prophets.
* National repentance.
* Divine restoration.
* The future outpouring of the Holy Spirit.
* God's final judgment of all nations.

Joel's prophecy concerning the Holy Spirit is directly quoted by the Apostle Peter in **Acts 2** on the Day of Pentecost, making Joel a significant bridge between the Old and New Testaments.

## Main Focus of the Book

The central message of Joel is:

> **God calls His people to wholehearted repentance, promises restoration to the repentant, and points forward to the coming Day of the LORD when He will judge the nations and establish His kingdom.**

Joel continues to speak powerfully to believers because it teaches that:

* God often uses difficult circumstances to awaken people spiritually.
* Genuine repentance involves the heart, not merely outward actions.
* God is able to restore what has been lost through sin or suffering.
* The gift of the Holy Spirit is part of God's redemptive plan.
* Believers should live in readiness for the return of Christ and the final Day of the Lord.

# Name of the Book

## Original Hebrew Name

**יוֹאֵל (Yōʾēl)**

---

## Root Word

The name is formed from two Hebrew elements:

* **Yo (יוֹ)** – a shortened form of **Yahweh (YHWH)**.
* **El (אֵל)** – God.

---

## Literal Meaning

**Joel** means:

> **"Yahweh is God"** or **"The LORD is God."**

His name itself is a declaration of faith, affirming that the God of Israel alone is the true and sovereign God.

---

## Why the Book Is Called by This Name

The book is named after **Joel, the son of Pethuel** (Joel 1:1), who faithfully delivered God's message to Judah.

Joel's name reflects the central theme of the book: **the LORD alone rules over nature, history, judgment, and salvation.**

---

# Author of the Book

## Traditional Author

The traditional author is **Joel, the son of Pethuel** (Joel 1:1).

Unlike prophets such as Isaiah or Jeremiah, Scripture provides almost no personal details about Joel. His ministry is known entirely through the message recorded in this book.

### Characteristics of Joel

Although little is known about his life, the book reveals that Joel was:

* A prophet deeply devoted to the Lord.
* A keen observer of nature, using the locust plague as a prophetic illustration.
* A preacher of heartfelt repentance.
* A prophet of hope who balanced warnings of judgment with promises of restoration.
* A visionary who looked beyond his own generation to the coming Day of the LORD and the outpouring of God's Spirit.

## Internal Evidence

The book opens with the statement:

> "The word of the LORD that came to Joel the son of Pethuel" (Joel 1:1).

The consistent literary style and detailed knowledge of Temple worship support the unity of the book.

## External Historical Evidence

Both Jewish and Christian tradition have consistently accepted Joel as the author.

No significant ancient tradition attributes the book to another writer.

## Scholarly Opinions

Most scholars agree that Joel wrote the book.

The primary scholarly discussion concerns **when** Joel lived rather than **who** wrote the book.

# Date Written

## Approximate Date

As discussed in Part 1, the exact date of Joel is uncertain. However, the **most widely accepted conservative view** places Joel's ministry around **835–800 BC**, during the early reign of **King Joash (Jehoash) of Judah**.

This makes Joel one of the **earliest writing prophets**, preceding Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, and Micah.

---

## Historical Timeline

Assuming the early-date view, Joel ministered during the following historical period:

| Approximate Date | Historical Event                                                       |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 931 BC           | Kingdom of Israel divides after Solomon's death.                       |
| 885–841 BC       | Ministry of Elijah.                                                    |
| 852–798 BC       | Ministry of Elisha.                                                    |
| 841 BC           | Jehu becomes king of Israel.                                           |
| 841–835 BC       | Athaliah rules Judah after seizing the throne.                         |
| 835 BC           | Joash crowned king through the leadership of Jehoiada the High Priest. |
| **835–800 BC**   | **Likely ministry of Joel.**                                           |
| 760 BC           | Amos begins his ministry.                                              |
| 755 BC           | Hosea begins his ministry.                                             |
| 740 BC           | Isaiah begins his ministry.                                            |
| 722 BC           | Assyria conquers the Northern Kingdom (Israel).                        |

---

## Why Many Scholars Favor an Early Date

Several observations support an early date:

* The Temple in Jerusalem is functioning normally (Joel 1:9, 13–14; 2:17).
* Joel frequently mentions **priests**, but never mentions a reigning king. This fits the period when **Jehoiada the High Priest** was the dominant leader during King Joash's childhood.
* Assyria, Babylon, and Persia are never mentioned, though they dominate later prophetic books.
* Joel condemns enemies such as **Philistia**, **Phoenicia (Tyre and Sidon)**, **Egypt**, and **Edom**, which were Judah's primary adversaries during the ninth century BC.

While certainty is impossible, these details strongly support the early-date view.

---

## Important Historical Background During the Writing Period

To appreciate Joel's message, it is important to understand Judah's recent history.

### The Divided Kingdom

After Solomon's death in **931 BC**, Israel divided into:

* **Northern Kingdom (Israel)** – Capital: Samaria.
* **Southern Kingdom (Judah)** – Capital: Jerusalem.

Joel ministered primarily to **Judah**.

---

### The Davidic Line Was Preserved

Shortly before Joel's ministry:

* Queen **Athaliah**, daughter of Ahab and Jezebel, attempted to destroy the royal descendants of David (2 Kings 11).
* The infant **Joash** was rescued and hidden in the Temple by **Jehosheba**.
* After six years, the high priest **Jehoiada** crowned Joash king.
* True worship of the Lord was restored.

God's preservation of David's line demonstrated His faithfulness to the covenant made with David (2 Samuel 7).

---

### The Locust Plague

During Joel's ministry, Judah experienced one of the worst natural disasters in its history.

A massive swarm of locusts devastated the land:

* Crops disappeared.
* Vineyards were destroyed.
* Olive trees were stripped bare.
* Grain offerings ceased because there was nothing left to offer.
* The nation faced famine and economic collapse.

Joel interpreted this event as **a warning from God**, calling the nation to repentance before an even greater judgment.

# Historical and Geographical Setting

## Historical Context

Joel ministered during a period when Judah was recovering politically and spiritually.

Although Temple worship had been restored under Jehoiada's leadership, the devastating locust plague threatened the nation's economy, worship, and morale.

Rather than treating the disaster as a random event, Joel recognized it as a call from God to national repentance.

---

## Political Background

If the early-date view is correct:

* King **Joash** was still young.
* The high priest **Jehoiada** exercised significant leadership.
* Judah enjoyed relative political stability compared to the Northern Kingdom.
* Foreign powers such as Assyria had not yet become the dominant threat they would later become.

Joel therefore focuses less on political events and more on the people's spiritual condition.

---

## Cultural Setting

Judah's society depended heavily on agriculture.

The destruction of crops meant:

* Hunger.
* Economic hardship.
* Interruption of Temple offerings.
* National mourning.

Agricultural festivals, which celebrated God's provision, could no longer be observed in the usual way.

This made Joel's message deeply personal for every household.

---

## Religious Condition of the People

The Temple was functioning, but Joel saw the need for deeper spiritual renewal.

He called for:

* Fasting.
* Prayer.
* Public repentance.
* Solemn assemblies.
* Hearts truly devoted to God.

His famous appeal captures this emphasis:

> **"Rend your heart, and not your garments."** (Joel 2:13)

God desired inward transformation rather than merely outward religious rituals.

# Key Verses

The Book of Joel contains some of the most significant prophetic passages in the Old Testament. Many of these verses are quoted or fulfilled in the New Testament.

---

## Joel 1:15

> **"Alas for the day! For the day of the LORD is at hand, and it shall come as destruction from the Almighty."**

### Explanation

This is the first major declaration of the **Day of the LORD** in Joel. While the immediate context is the devastating locust plague, Joel uses it to point toward a future day when God will intervene decisively in human history through judgment and salvation.

### Why It Is Significant

* Introduces the central theme of the book.
* Connects present judgment with future prophetic events.
* Becomes a foundational theme for later prophets such as Isaiah, Amos, Zephaniah, Zechariah, and Malachi.

---

## Joel 2:12–13

> **"'Yet even now,' declares the LORD, 'return to Me with all your heart, with fasting, with weeping, and with mourning; and rend your hearts and not your garments.' Return to the LORD your God, for He is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love, and He relents over disaster."**

### Explanation

God's call is for genuine inward repentance rather than mere outward expressions of sorrow. In ancient Israel, tearing one's garments was a traditional sign of grief, but God desired transformed hearts instead of external rituals.

### Why It Is Significant

* Emphasizes sincere repentance.
* Reveals God's gracious and compassionate character.
* Demonstrates that mercy is available to those who truly return to Him.

---

## Joel 2:25

> **"I will restore to you the years that the swarming locust has eaten..."**

### Explanation

After repentance comes God's promise of restoration. Although the locust plague had caused great devastation, God promised to restore His people and renew their land.

### Why It Is Significant

* Illustrates God's power to redeem what has been lost.
* Offers hope to believers who have experienced seasons of spiritual or personal loss.
* Highlights God's faithfulness after repentance.

---

## Joel 2:28–29

> **"And it shall come to pass afterward, that I will pour out My Spirit on all flesh..."**

### Explanation

Joel foretells a future outpouring of God's Spirit upon all kinds of people—young and old, men and women, servants and free. This prophecy points beyond Joel's own day to God's expanding work among His people.

### Why It Is Significant

* Quoted by the Apostle Peter in **Acts 2:16–21** as fulfilled in connection with the Day of Pentecost.
* Marks a significant stage in God's redemptive plan.
* Demonstrates that God's Spirit would be given broadly rather than only to selected leaders.

---

## Joel 2:32

> **"And it shall come to pass that everyone who calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved."**

### Explanation

Joel declares that salvation is available to all who call upon the Lord in faith.

### Why It Is Significant

* Quoted by the Apostle Paul in **Romans 10:13** in explaining the universal offer of salvation through Jesus Christ.
* Reveals God's gracious invitation to all who seek Him.

---

## Joel 3:16

> **"The LORD roars from Zion... But the LORD is a refuge to His people."**

### Explanation

God is portrayed both as Judge of the nations and Protector of His covenant people.

### Why It Is Significant

* Highlights God's justice and His care for those who belong to Him.
* Balances themes of judgment and security.

## Chapter 2 – The Day of the LORD, Repentance, and Restoration

### Main Events

Joel sounds a trumpet in Zion, warning that the **Day of the LORD** is approaching.

He describes an overwhelming invading army using imagery similar to the earlier locust plague. Many interpreters understand this as both a continuation of the locust imagery and a prophecy of future military invasion, illustrating God's coming judgment.

Despite the warning, God graciously invites His people:

> "Return to Me with all your heart."

The people are instructed to gather together in fasting and prayer.

The priests are to stand between the porch and the altar, interceding for the nation.

When the people respond in repentance, God promises to:

* Remove their reproach.
* Restore the land.
* Send rain.
* Rebuild the harvest.
* Replace what the locusts had consumed.
* Live among His people once again.

Joel then looks beyond his own generation and announces a future outpouring of God's Spirit upon all kinds of people. This prophecy reaches its New Testament fulfillment as Peter explains the events of Pentecost (Acts 2:16–21).

The chapter concludes by declaring that **everyone who calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved**, emphasizing God's gracious invitation.

* God delights in showing mercy.

* God's Spirit empowers His people for faithful service.

## Chapter 3 – The Judgment of the Nations and the Future Blessing of God's People

### Main Events

Joel concludes his prophecy by looking beyond the immediate future to the ultimate **Day of the LORD**.

God announces that He will gather all nations into the **Valley of Jehoshaphat** ("Yahweh judges") to hold them accountable for how they treated His covenant people.

The nations are judged because they:

* Scattered Israel among the nations.
* Divided God's land.
* Sold His people into slavery.
* Oppressed Judah and Jerusalem.

Joel then uses harvest imagery:

* **"Put in the sickle, for the harvest is ripe."**
* **"The winepress is full."**

These images portray the completeness of God's judgment.

The chapter ends with a beautiful contrast:

* God judges rebellious nations.
* God protects His people.
* Jerusalem becomes secure.
* The land flourishes with abundance.
* God's presence remains with His people forever.

# Theological Themes

## God's Character

* Holy.
* Just.
* Merciful.
* Compassionate.
* Sovereign over creation and history.
* Faithful to His covenant.

---

## Salvation

Joel emphasizes that salvation belongs to those who call upon the Lord in faith.

God initiates restoration through His grace.

---

## Covenant

God remains faithful to His covenant with Israel despite periods of discipline.

His covenant faithfulness anticipates the New Covenant fulfilled in Christ.

---

## Faith

Faith is demonstrated through repentance, obedience, and trust in God's promises.

---

## Grace

God graciously invites sinners to return.

His mercy follows genuine repentance.

Restoration is entirely an act of divine grace.

---

## Judgment

The Day of the LORD includes:

* Judgment upon sin.
* Vindication of God's people.
* Justice for the nations.
* Final establishment of God's kingdom.

---

## Holiness

God calls His people to wholehearted devotion.

External religious practices must flow from transformed hearts.

---

## Kingdom of God

Joel anticipates God's future reign when:

* Evil is judged.
* God's people dwell securely.
* The Lord dwells among His people.

---

## Worship

True worship includes:

* Repentance.
* Obedience.
* Reverence.
* Dependence upon God.

---

## Sin and Redemption

Sin brings devastation.

Repentance brings forgiveness.

God restores what sin has destroyed.

---

## Obedience

God desires inward obedience rather than outward ritual alone.

# Summary of the Book

The Book of Joel begins with a devastating locust plague that serves as both a historical event and a prophetic warning. Joel calls Judah to wholehearted repentance, reminding the people that outward rituals are meaningless without transformed hearts.

The prophecy then expands to reveal the coming **Day of the LORD**, when God will judge evil, vindicate His people, and establish His righteous kingdom. At the center of the book stands God's gracious promise to restore what has been lost and to pour out His Spirit upon His people.

Joel's message reaches beyond his own generation, pointing to Pentecost, the worldwide proclamation of the Gospel, and the future return of Christ.

---

## Main Theological Message

**God is both the righteous Judge and the gracious Restorer. Those who sincerely repent experience His mercy, while all people are called to prepare for the coming Day of the LORD.**

---

## Spiritual Takeaway

* Repent wholeheartedly.
* Trust God's mercy.
* Depend upon the Holy Spirit.
* Live in readiness for Christ's return.
* Find hope in God's promise of restoration.

---

## Importance in the Bible

Joel provides one of Scripture's clearest presentations of:

* The Day of the LORD.
* National repentance.
* Divine restoration.
* The outpouring of the Holy Spirit.
* God's final judgment and kingdom.

His prophecy forms an important bridge between the Old Testament prophets and the New Testament fulfillment of God's redemptive plan.

---

# Additional Important Information

## Literary Structure

Joel is carefully arranged in four movements:

1. **The Historical Crisis** (Chapter 1).
2. **The Call to Repentance** (2:1–17).
3. **God's Restoration and the Promise of the Spirit** (2:18–32).
4. **The Future Judgment and Kingdom** (Chapter 3).

The movement from judgment to restoration is central to the book's message.

---

## Important Symbols

| Symbol           | Meaning                                                     |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Locusts          | Divine judgment and a warning of greater future judgment.   |
| Trumpet (Shofar) | Call to repentance and announcement of the Day of the LORD. |
| Fire and Drought | Consequences of sin and divine discipline.                  |
| Rain             | God's blessing, provision, and restoration.                 |
| Harvest          | God's judgment and the gathering of people.                 |
| Wine and Oil     | Joy, abundance, and covenant blessing.                      |
| Zion             | God's dwelling place and the center of His kingdom.         |

---

## Covenants

Joel reflects the **Mosaic Covenant**, where obedience brings blessing and disobedience brings discipline (Deuteronomy 28). At the same time, his promise of the Spirit anticipates the blessings of the **New Covenant**, fulfilled through Jesus Christ.

---

## Historical Questions

The primary scholarly discussion concerns the date of Joel's ministry. Regardless of whether one adopts an early or late date, the theological message of the book remains unchanged: **God calls His people to repentance, promises restoration, and announces His future reign.**

---

## Hebrew Insights

Two important Hebrew expressions in Joel are:

* **Yôm YHWH (יוֹם יְהוָה)** — **"The Day of the LORD."** This phrase refers to decisive moments when God acts in judgment and salvation, culminating in the final establishment of His kingdom.

* **Shuv (שׁוּב)** — **"Return."** Joel's repeated call to "return" emphasizes wholehearted repentance and renewed covenant fellowship with God.

---

## Connections to the Gospel

Joel points to the Gospel in several ways:

* The promise of the Holy Spirit is fulfilled through Christ after His resurrection and ascension (Acts 2).
* The invitation that "everyone who calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved" finds its fullest expression in the proclamation of salvation through Jesus Christ (Romans 10:13).
* The coming Day of the LORD anticipates Christ's second coming, when He will judge evil and establish His eternal kingdom.

---

## Archaeological Insights

While no archaeological discovery can be identified with the specific locust plague described by Joel, ancient records from the Near East document devastating locust invasions that caused widespread famine and economic collapse. These accounts help modern readers appreciate the severity of the disaster Joel describes.

---

## Important Study Notes for Bible Teachers

* Distinguish between the **historical locust plague** and its **prophetic significance**.
* Trace the theme of the **Day of the LORD** throughout Scripture.
* Compare Joel's prophecy of the Spirit with its application in **Acts 2**.
* Emphasize that God's judgment is always balanced by His invitation to repentance and His promise of restoration.
* Show how Joel prepares readers for both the first and second comings of Christ.

---

# Conclusion

The Book of Joel is a powerful reminder that God speaks through both His Word and the events of history. What began as a devastating locust plague became a timeless message about repentance, hope, and God's sovereign purposes.

Joel calls every generation to return to the Lord with sincere hearts, assuring us that He is "gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love." His prophecy reaches beyond ancient Judah to the coming of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost and ultimately to the return of Jesus Christ, when the Day of the LORD will reach its final fulfillment.

For pastors, teachers, students, and every believer, Joel offers enduring encouragement: **God disciplines to restore, empowers by His Spirit, and will one day make all things right. Those who call upon the name of the Lord can live with confidence, hope, and joyful expectation of His coming Kingdom.**
